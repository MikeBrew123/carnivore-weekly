What “net carbs” really means — and why it decides your whole day

Fiber, sugar alcohols, and the small piece of arithmetic that quietly determines whether a food fits your keto target.

S
Sarah ChenHealth & science writer
Published May 28, 2026
8 min read
article hero · 16:7

Most “low-carb” labels are counting something different than your body does.

Advertisement728 × 90leaderboard

If you've spent any time on a keto diet, you've run into the phrase “net carbs” — usually in big, friendly letters on a package that's quietly hoping you won't do the math. The good news is the math is simple. The better news is that once you understand it, almost every confusing food label suddenly makes sense.

Net carbs are the carbohydrates your body actually digests and turns into glucose. Not every gram on the label does that — and the gap between “total carbs” and “net carbs” is exactly where keto lives or dies.

The one piece of arithmetic that matters

Here's the whole formula. Take the total carbohydrates on a label, then subtract the parts that don't raise your blood sugar:

The net-carb formula
  • Net carbs = total carbohydrates − fiber − sugar alcohols. Those last two largely pass through without spiking glucose.
  • Fiber is a carbohydrate you can't digest — subtract all of it.
  • Sugar alcohols are partly absorbed — subtract half as a safe rule of thumb (erythritol is the exception; subtract it all).

That's it. Everything else is just applying it carefully and not getting fooled by marketing.

Why fiber gets a free pass

Fiber is technically a carbohydrate, but your small intestine lacks the enzymes to break most of it down. It either feeds the bacteria in your gut or passes straight through. Either way, it doesn't flood your bloodstream with glucose — so it doesn't count against your ketogenic ceiling.

A cup of spinach and a slice of bread can have similar “total carbs.” Only one of them will keep you in ketosis.

This is why leafy greens, avocados, and chia seeds are keto staples despite showing carbs on the label: most of those carbs are fiber. Subtract it, and what's left barely registers.

Sugar alcohols: the tricky ones

Sugar alcohols (erythritol, xylitol, maltitol and friends) are where “net carb” labels get optimistic. Manufacturers love to subtract 100% of them, but they're not all equal. Erythritol genuinely passes through almost untouched. Maltitol, on the other hand, raises blood sugar quite a bit — subtracting all of it is wishful thinking.

FoodTotal carbsFiberNet carbs
Avocado (½)9 g7 g1 g
Spinach (2 cups)2 g1.4 g0.6 g
Almonds (1 oz)6 g3.5 g2.5 g
“Keto” bar w/ maltitol22 g3 g~12 g*

*Label may claim 2g. Maltitol's real glycemic impact means you should count roughly half of it.

How this plays out on your daily target

Your KetoDial macro target gives you a net-carb ceiling — often around 20–25 g per day. Spend it on whole foods and you'll feel like you're eating a lot. Spend it on a single “keto” pastry made with maltitol and you might blow through it without realizing.

  • Lean on the green-light foods: leafy greens, avocado, eggs, meat, fish, olive oil.
  • Treat packaged “keto” snacks as occasional, and trust erythritol over maltitol.
  • When a label seems too good to be true, recompute it yourself with the formula above.

The bottom line

Net carbs aren't a loophole — they're a more accurate way of counting what your body actually does with food. Learn the formula once, apply a little skepticism to sugar alcohols, and you'll never have to trust a misleading front-of-package claim again.
