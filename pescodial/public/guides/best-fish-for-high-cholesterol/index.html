The Best Fish for High Cholesterol (and the Ones to Go Easy On)

By Sarah, the PescoDial writer

Updated October 20, 2026

I'm not a doctor. This is general information, not medical advice.

Your doctor says your LDL is too high. Maybe you're on a statin and the number still isn't where she'd like it. Then comes the advice everyone agrees on: eat more fish. But at the seafood counter it gets confusing fast. Is salmon better than cod? Does canned tuna count? And what about the Friday fish and chips you love?

Here's the honest picture. Fish helps your cholesterol mostly through what it replaces on your plate and how it's cooked, not because one special species melts LDL away. What Actually Moves LDL (and Where Fish Fits)

For most people, saturated fat is the part of the diet that pushes LDL up the most. The American Heart Association recommends keeping it under 6% of your daily calories. Most of it comes from fatty and processed meats, butter, cheese and cream.

Most fish is low in saturated fat. Even oily fish like salmon carries most of its fat as unsaturated fat. The AHA's 2017 advisory on dietary fats concluded that replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fats lowers LDL and is linked with less heart disease.

So the biggest win usually isn't the fish on its own. It's the swap: salmon instead of a ribeye, a tuna salad instead of a ham sandwich, baked cod instead of sausages. The AHA recommends 2 servings of fish a week, particularly fatty fish, with a serving being 3 oz cooked (about 85 g, or roughly 3/4 cup of flaked fish).

Research shows general patterns, and your body may respond differently. Some people see a real LDL drop from changes like these, and some see very little, especially when genetics drive the number. That's exactly why food works alongside a statin, never instead of one.

The Best Fish for High Cholesterol

Every fish below is on the FDA and EPA "Best Choices" list, the lowest-mercury group.

Fatty fish or lean fish?

Both belong in your week. A 3 oz serving of cooked farmed Atlantic salmon has about 2 g of saturated fat, while the same amount of cooked cod has about 0.1 g. For comparison, 3 oz of cooked 80% lean ground beef has about 5.5 g. Salmon's extra fat comes with omega-3, so it earns its place. Wild salmon such as sockeye tends to be leaner than farmed.

What about omega-3?

Fatty fish is where EPA and DHA, the omega-3 fats, come from. Their best-known effect on blood fats is lowering triglycerides rather than LDL, and the AHA's 2018 seafood advisory linked eating fish 1 to 2 times a week with lower risk of heart disease, especially when it replaces less healthy foods. Shrimp and lean white fish aren't meaningful sources, so lean on the oily fish in the top half of the table for this. Food first: if you're wondering about fish oil pills, that's a conversation for your doctor or pharmacist, not the supplement aisle.

The Fish to Go Easy On

Most of this list is about how fish is prepared, not the fish itself.

  • Fried and battered fish. Fish and chips, fish sticks and breaded fillets. The batter soaks up frying oil and adds a lot of calories.
  • Butter, cream and cheese sauces. Alfredo, creamy chowder, or a tuna melt piled with cheese. These bring back the saturated fat you were trying to swap out.
  • Smoked, salted and some canned fish, if you watch your blood pressure. Smoked salmon can carry more than ten times the sodium of cooked fresh salmon: about 570 mg in 3 oz of smoked chinook, against about 50 mg in 3 oz of cooked chinook. Standard advice is under 2,300 mg of sodium a day, and if you have high blood pressure, your doctor's target comes first. Look for "no salt added" or "low sodium" on canned fish, and compare labels.
  • Higher-mercury fish. Skip the FDA and EPA "Choices to Avoid": king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, shark, swordfish, bigeye tuna, and tilefish from the Gulf of Mexico. Albacore (white) tuna sits in the middle "Good Choices" group, so canned light tuna is the lower-mercury everyday pick.

Shrimp is its own question because of its dietary cholesterol, and I've covered it in is shrimp bad for cholesterol.

How to Cook Fish So It Stays Heart-Friendly

The cooking method matters as much as the species. Here's what I've seen work for people who want fish to be easy, not a project.

  • Bake or roast. A sheet pan, a little olive oil, lemon and herbs.
  • Broil or grill. Salmon and mackerel handle direct heat. Wrap delicate white fish in a foil packet.
  • Poach or steam. Gentle and nearly fat-free.
  • Pan-sear in olive oil. A spoonful, not a puddle, and lemon instead of a butter sauce.
  • Air fry. A light coating gets you the crunch without the deep fryer.
  • Season without leaning on salt. Garlic, dill, paprika, black pepper, lemon zest and fresh parsley do a lot of work.

One small tool helps more than any recipe: an instant-read thermometer. The FDA advises cooking most seafood to 145°F, and checking keeps you from overcooking it.

Making Fish a Habit, Not a Chore

The fish that helps is the fish you actually eat, week after week.

  • Cook twice, eat twice. Bake two salmon fillets and put the second one on a salad the next day.
  • Keep a tired-night shelf. Canned salmon, sardines and light tuna mean dinner is ten minutes away.
  • Buy frozen. Frozen fillets are usually cheaper than fresh and keep for months.
  • Put fish days on the calendar. Two set nights a week beats a vague plan to "eat more fish."

If you'd like the week laid out for you, the 7-day pescatarian meal plan builds fish into most days with portions already worked out.

If you take a statin or other cholesterol medicine: please don't stop it, skip doses, or change how you take it because of anything in this guide. Food works alongside a statin, never instead of one. The same goes for blood-pressure medicine, blood thinners such as warfarin, and diabetes medicine, which can all be affected when you change how you eat or start a supplement. Check with your doctor or pharmacist before changing your medicine, starting fish oil or any other supplement, or making a big change in how you eat.

When to Talk to Your Doctor

Talk to your doctor if your LDL hasn't moved after a few months of eating differently, or if you have familial high cholesterol or a strong family history of early heart disease. Ask whether a repeat lipid panel makes sense a few months after you change how you eat, so you can see how your own body responds.

Check with your doctor or pharmacist before trying fish oil or any omega-3 pill, especially if you take a blood thinner. If you take diabetes medicine and you're eating less to lose weight, ask how to watch for low blood sugar. If you've ever had itching, swelling or trouble breathing after eating fish, avoid it and get checked for a fish allergy. And chest pain, pressure, or sudden shortness of breath is an emergency. Call 911 or your local emergency number.

The bottom line: choose the fish in the table, let them replace fattier meats, and cook them simply. Go easy on batter, butter, cream and salty smoked fish, and let your bloodwork and your doctor tell you how it's working.
