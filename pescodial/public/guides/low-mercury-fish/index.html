Low Mercury Fish: How to Spot the Safest Picks When You Shop

By Sarah, the PescoDial writer

Updated November 3, 2026

I'm not a doctor. This is general information, not medical advice.

You're standing at the seafood counter, or halfway down the canned aisle, and you want fish that's low in mercury. Then the labels start working against you. "Sea bass" can mean two very different fish. "Ahi" might be one of two tunas, and one of them is on the FDA's list to skip. "Black cod" isn't cod at all.

This guide is about that moment: which fish run lowest in mercury, how to spot them under the names you'll actually see, and what to ask when you're ordering out. How often to eat fish is its own question, and I covered it in how much fish per week after 60. The short version is 2 to 3 servings a week (about 8 to 12 ounces) from the lowest-mercury group, which the FDA says anyone can follow. If you'd like your own calorie and protein numbers before you write the shopping list, the free PescoDial calculator gives you a starting point.

Pregnancy and young children have their own guidance, and the FDA's Advice about Eating Fish page is the place to read it.

Why Some Fish Carry More Mercury

Let me explain the why first, because it makes the rest easier to remember. The mercury in fish is mostly methylmercury, and how much a fish carries depends on what it eats, how long it lives and how high it sits in the food chain. Small fish that eat plankton carry very little. Big, long-lived fish that eat other fish carry the most. That's why sardines and anchovies sit near the bottom of the FDA's measurements and swordfish and shark sit near the top.

So here's a quick rule for the counter: small, short-lived and low on the food chain usually means low mercury. A big predator sold as a thick steak usually means higher. It isn't perfect (cod is a fair-sized fish and still lands in the lowest group), but it's a good start.

One more thing that surprises people. Mercury is found throughout the flesh of the fish, so cleaning, trimming or cooking won't reduce it. The species decides it, not the kitchen.

Two things jump out. First, everything from scallops down through canned light tuna sits in the FDA's Best Choices group. Pick the ones you like. Second, look at the gap at the bottom. Swordfish averages more than 70 times the mercury of sardines. That's why it sits on the skip list with king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, shark, bigeye tuna and Gulf of Mexico tilefish, rather than being something you work around with smaller portions.

Names That Trip People Up

Same word on the sign, very different fish.

  • Mackerel. Atlantic mackerel and Pacific chub mackerel are Best Choices. Spanish mackerel is a Good Choice. King mackerel is on the avoid list. On a can, look for the species on the label.
  • Sea bass. Black sea bass is a Best Choice. Chilean sea bass is a different fish altogether, Patagonian toothfish, and it sits in Good Choices with a much higher average of about 0.354 ppm.
  • Cod. Cod is a Best Choice. "Black cod" is sablefish, which isn't a cod at all, and it's a Good Choice.
  • Ahi and tuna steaks. "Ahi" can mean yellowfin, a Good Choice, or bigeye, which is on the avoid list. If a menu or a sushi counter says ahi or tuna steak and nobody can tell you which, order something else.
  • Tilefish. Gulf of Mexico tilefish is on the avoid list. Atlantic tilefish is a Good Choice. If the sign just says tilefish, ask where it came from.
  • Dolphin. On a menu this means mahi mahi (dolphinfish), a fish, not the animal. It's a Good Choice.

At the Fish Counter

Here's what I've seen work. When I do my mom's groceries on Wednesdays, she reaches for sole and frozen cod, and both sit at the low end of that table without her ever having to think about it. The habit worth borrowing is looking for the species name, not just "white fish" or "seafood medley." If the label is vague, ask. A good fish counter will know.

Frozen counts the same as fresh. The FDA's lists go by species, not by whether the fish was frozen, so bags of frozen salmon, cod, pollock, shrimp and scallops are an easy way to keep Best Choices in the freezer. Farmed or wild doesn't change the picture for salmon either: the FDA lists salmon as a Best Choice without splitting the two.

In the Canned Aisle

Canned salmon, sardines, Atlantic mackerel, anchovies, herring and clams are all Best Choices, and they're some of the easiest ways to get fish on the table without cooking. Canned tuna is the one to read closely. "Light" or skipjack tuna is a Best Choice. "White" or albacore is a Good Choice, with about three times the mercury of light.

The other thing to check on a can is salt. Canned and smoked fish can run high in sodium, so look at the Nutrition Facts panel, reach for "no salt added" when it's on the shelf, and keep the whole day under 2,300 mg of sodium, the standard guidance.

If you take medicine: choosing a lower-mercury fish is a food choice, but a few medicines are worth a quick conversation. If you're on a blood thinner such as warfarin, take questions about eating more fish, or about fish oil, to your pharmacist, who knows your full medicine list. If you take blood-pressure medicine, the salt in canned and smoked fish is the part to ask about. If you take a statin or diabetes medicine, your doctor or pharmacist can tell you whether a change in how you eat matters for you. Check with them before you change anything. This is not for everyone. Talk to your doctor first if you have high blood pressure, kidney or heart problems, diabetes, are pregnant, or take medicine.

Ordering at a Restaurant

Eating out is the hardest, because menus often say "catch of the day" or "market fish." Up here in Whistler the restaurants are built for visitors, and I've gotten comfortable just asking the server, "What kind of fish is that tonight?" Most know, or will ask the kitchen.

Easy low-mercury orders include grilled or baked salmon, fish and chips made with cod or haddock, fish tacos with pollock or cod, shrimp, scallops and crab cakes. The orders worth a second question are thick steaks (swordfish and tuna), anything called ahi, Chilean sea bass, and "white tuna" on a sushi menu. Ask which fish it is.

When to Talk to Your Doctor

The FDA calls lower-mercury fish a good choice for everyone. Get checked by your doctor if you notice any of these:

  • New pins and needles or numbness in your hands, feet or around your mouth
  • New trouble with balance, walking or coordination
  • Loss of side vision, or hearing changes you can't explain
  • New muscle weakness or trouble with your speech
  • Years of eating high-mercury fish such as swordfish, shark or bigeye tuna often, and you're worried about it. Ask whether testing makes sense for you.

Swelling, hives or trouble breathing after eating fish or shellfish is an emergency. Call 911 or your local emergency number.

The Bottom Line

Low-mercury fish isn't a short, fussy list. It's most of what's in the case: salmon, sardines, shrimp, cod, pollock, tilapia, haddock, sole, trout, Atlantic mackerel, herring and light tuna. Read the species, not the nickname, watch the look-alikes, treat frozen and canned as equal to fresh, and ask when the menu is vague. Then spend your attention on fish you'll actually enjoy.
