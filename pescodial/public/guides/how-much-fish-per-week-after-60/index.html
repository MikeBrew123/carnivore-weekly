How Much Fish Per Week After 60? Mercury vs. Heart Benefits

By Sarah, the PescoDial writer

Updated October 30, 2026

I'm not a doctor. This is general information, not medical advice.

You keep hearing two things about fish that seem to pull in opposite directions. One, it's one of the best things you can eat for your heart after 60. Two, watch the mercury. So how much fish a week is actually safe, and does the mercury worry cancel out the benefit? It doesn't, for most people, but the right answer depends on which fish you're eating, not just how often.

This isn't a list of every omega-3 source or a full meal plan. I've covered those elsewhere. This is the narrower question: how many servings a week, from which list, and what mercury actually does to tip that balance. If you want a sense of where your own calorie and protein numbers should land first, the free PescoDial calculator gives you a starting point. It's based on your age, height and weight.

The Short Answer

For most adults, including most people over 60, the FDA and EPA's joint fish advisory supports eating 2 to 3 servings of fish a week. That's roughly 8 to 12 ounces total. The condition is that most of it comes from the "Best Choices" list, not the small group of fish known to run high in mercury. That advisory was written for women who are or may become pregnant and for young children. But the FDA says anyone can use the same chart. The American Heart Association separately recommends at least 2 servings of fish a week, favoring fatty fish for the heart benefit. A serving is about 3 ounces cooked, roughly 3/4 cup of flaked fish.

Put those together and you land in the same place. For most people past 60, 2 to 3 fish meals a week, built mostly around fatty, low-mercury fish, is a reasonable target. That's a floor you can comfortably clear, not a ceiling you have to watch.

Why Mercury Is the Tradeoff At All

Mercury gets into the ocean mostly from industrial pollution, and it builds up in fish over their lifetime. It concentrates most in larger, longer-lived predator species that eat a lot of smaller fish. A small amount of methylmercury, the form found in seafood, isn't dangerous on its own. The concern is repeated exposure from the same high-mercury fish, week after week, over years.

The FDA and EPA's advisory was built with pregnant women, people who may become pregnant, and young children in mind. Mercury is most clearly harmful to a developing nervous system. That's not most readers here. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans' separate advice for general adults is simply to eat at least 8 ounces of fish a week. But the FDA says everyone is welcome to follow the same chart, and it's still worth following. Nobody benefits from steadily accumulating mercury. And the fish on the "avoid" list don't offer anything nutritionally that a Best Choice doesn't already give you.

Best Choices: What to Build Your Week Around

These fish sit in the FDA and EPA's "Best Choices" category. They're low enough in mercury that most people can eat 2 to 3 servings a week without concern. They also happen to include the fish richest in the omega-3s that heart research is actually talking about:

  • Salmon
  • Sardines
  • Atlantic mackerel
  • Herring
  • Trout
  • Shrimp
  • Cod
  • Tilapia
  • Light canned tuna
  • Catfish, pollock, and most other common grocery-store and freezer-aisle fish

Salmon, sardines, mackerel, herring and trout carry the most omega-3s per serving. That's the piece doing most of the work for your heart. I go through those numbers in detail in which fish are actually high in omega-3, so I won't repeat the full breakdown here. Shrimp, cod, tilapia and light tuna are low in mercury and fine to eat often. They just aren't a meaningful omega-3 source. Think of them as your lean protein meals rather than your heart-fat meals.

A Fish Worth Watching, Not Avoiding

Albacore, or white, tuna sits in a middle category the FDA and EPA call "Good Choices." It's not on the avoid list, but the guidance is stricter than for Best Choices fish: 1 serving a week, and no other fish that week. That's because albacore runs roughly three times higher in mercury than canned light tuna. If you already eat light tuna regularly, that's the simpler choice to lean on instead.

Choices to Avoid

A small group of fish consistently test high in mercury. The FDA and EPA recommend skipping them rather than working around a serving limit:

  • King mackerel
  • Marlin
  • Orange roughy
  • Shark
  • Swordfish
  • Bigeye tuna
  • Tilefish from the Gulf of Mexico

None of these are common weeknight fish for most people, so this list is easier to follow than it looks. One mix-up is worth remembering. King mackerel is on the avoid list. Regular Atlantic mackerel, the kind in most cans and fillets at the grocery store, is a Best Choice. Read the label rather than going by the word "mackerel" alone.

So Does the Heart Benefit Win?

For the fish most people actually reach for, yes. The heart research behind the AHA's 2-servings-a-week recommendation was built on the same fatty fish that sit on the Best Choices list: salmon, sardines, mackerel, herring, trout. You're not trading mercury exposure for heart benefit with those fish. The serving counts that earn the benefit are the same ones the mercury guidance already clears.

The tradeoff only gets real if your weekly fish habit leans on something from the avoid list, or on albacore tuna several times a week. For almost everyone else, 2 to 3 Best Choices servings a week is simply good eating. The mercury risk is low enough that it isn't the thing to spend your attention on. Your attention is better spent on building the habit at all. Then make sure some of those servings are the fattier fish, rather than all lean white fish and shrimp.

A Note on Canned and Smoked Fish

Canned salmon and sardines are some of the easiest ways to hit 2 to 3 servings a week without cooking much, and they're on the Best Choices list. The one thing worth a glance is sodium, since canned and smoked fish can run higher than fresh. Per USDA FoodData Central, a 3-ounce serving of canned salmon or canned sardines typically runs around 300 to 400 mg of sodium. Smoked salmon runs noticeably higher, often 500 to 650 mg. Check the label and choose a no-salt-added version when it's available. Keep your overall day under 2,300 mg of sodium. That's the upper ceiling in the current Dietary Guidelines for Americans, not a target to aim for.

Before you change how much fish you eat: food first is the safest place to start. For most people, 2 to 3 servings a week from the Best Choices list is reasonable to adjust toward on their own. If you're on a blood thinner such as warfarin, food changes need your pharmacist's input, since several foods common in a pescatarian pattern can affect how the medicine works. If you take a statin, blood-pressure medicine or diabetes medicine, talk with your doctor or pharmacist before making a significant change to how you eat. This is not for everyone. Talk to your doctor first if you have high blood pressure, kidney or heart problems, diabetes, are pregnant, or take medicine.

When to Talk to Your Doctor

A change in how much fish you eat is a reasonable moment to also check in on your body generally. Call your doctor, or get checked soon, if you notice any of the following:

  • Numbness, tingling, or a pins-and-needles feeling in your hands or feet that's new
  • Trouble with balance, coordination, or vision that's new or getting worse
  • Chest pain, pressure or tightness, or pain that spreads to your arm, neck or jaw
  • Sudden shortness of breath, especially with little activity
  • Swelling, hives, or trouble breathing shortly after eating fish or shellfish
  • A heartbeat that feels fast, irregular or pounding

Chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, or a severe allergic reaction is an emergency. Call 911 or your local emergency number rather than waiting for an appointment.

The Bottom Line

For most people past 60, 2 to 3 servings of fish a week from the Best Choices list is both the mercury-safe amount and the amount that earns the heart benefit. They're built from the same research. Skip the small list of fish to avoid, go easy on albacore tuna, and lean toward the fattier Best Choices when you can: salmon, sardines, mackerel, herring, trout. If you're curious whether this way of eating fits your week more broadly, I covered that question directly in is pescatarian eating healthy after 50. Then build the rest of the week around fish you'll actually keep eating.
