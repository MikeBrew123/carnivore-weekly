Fish High in Omega-3: What to Eat Each Week, Food First

By Sarah, the PescoDial writer

Updated October 23, 2026

I'm not a doctor. This is general information, not medical advice.

You want more omega-3s for your heart, you'd rather get them from food than from another bottle on the counter, and you're standing at the fish counter wondering which of these fillets actually count. That's the right instinct. Most of the fish people buy most often, like cod, tilapia and shrimp, are lovely lean protein, but they carry very little of the omega-3s heart research is talking about. A handful of oily fish do nearly all the work.

So this guide isn't a long list to memorize. It's a short list of fish that matter, how much of them counts as a serving, and how to fit them into an ordinary week without cooking fish every night. If you're also trying to lose a little weight for your heart, the free PescoDial calculator gives you a daily protein and calorie target, so your fish meals fit your day on purpose.

Which Omega-3s Your Heart Research Is Talking About

When doctors and heart researchers talk about fish and omega-3s, they mean two fats called EPA and DHA. Your body can make only small amounts of them on its own, so food is the practical way to get them, and oily fish are by far the richest food source.

You'll also see "omega-3" on bags of flaxseed, chia and walnuts. That's a different omega-3 called ALA. It's a healthy fat and worth eating, but it isn't the same as the EPA and DHA in fish, so a handful of walnuts doesn't stand in for a salmon dinner.

Here's how common fish compare. Amounts are approximate EPA plus DHA in a 3 oz cooked serving, about the size of a deck of cards. Exact numbers vary with species, season, and whether the fish is wild or farmed, so treat these as rough guides rather than labels.

The pattern is simple. The fish that taste rich and leave a little oil on the plate are the ones with the omega-3s. Mild white fish and shellfish are worth eating for protein, but they won't move your omega-3 intake much.

How Much Fish Is Enough?

The American Heart Association recommends eating fish at least 2 times a week, with a serving being 3 oz cooked, or about 3/4 cup of flaked fish, and it points people toward fatty fish in particular. A 2018 AHA science advisory reviewed the research and supported one to two seafood meals a week as part of a heart-healthy pattern, especially when fish takes the place of less healthy foods on the plate.

That's the part people miss. Part of the benefit seems to come from what the fish replaces. A salmon fillet instead of a fried pork chop or a plate of processed meat does two good things at once.

Research shows general patterns, and your own needs may differ. Use two oily-fish meals a week as a starting point you can actually keep, not a test you pass or fail.

A Normal Week With Omega-3 Fish

Here's what I've seen work: pick two fixed fish nights and one easy fish lunch, then stop thinking about it. You don't need fancy recipes. You need a few reliable defaults.

Make it easy on yourself

  • Frozen counts. Individually frozen salmon or trout fillets go from freezer to oven on a weeknight, and they're often cheaper than fresh.
  • Canned counts. Canned salmon and sardines are some of the most affordable omega-3 food you can buy. The soft bones are edible and add calcium.
  • Watch the salt in canned and smoked fish. Smoked salmon and some canned fish are high in sodium. Check the label, look for low-sodium options, and stay within the general guide of under 2,300 mg of sodium a day, or your doctor's lower target if you have one.
  • Cook it simply. Baked, broiled, grilled, poached or pan-seared in a little olive oil keeps the meal heart-friendly. Deep-fried and battered fish adds a lot of fat and calories, and a fish stick isn't the same meal as a salmon fillet.
  • Mild-tasting starters. If oily fish feels strong, start with farmed salmon or rainbow trout, which are milder, before working up to sardines and mackerel.

What About Shrimp, Cod and Tilapia?

Keep eating them if you enjoy them. They're lean, high in protein and low in mercury. They just aren't where your omega-3s come from, so count them as protein meals and let your oily-fish meals carry the omega-3 job. If shrimp worries you for a different reason, I wrote about whether shrimp is bad for cholesterol separately.

If your main goal is lowering LDL rather than getting more omega-3s, that's a slightly different question, and fish mostly helps there by replacing fattier meats.

A Quick Word on Mercury

Every fish in the table above sits on the FDA and EPA "Best Choices" list for mercury. A few large fish are on the list to avoid: king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, shark, swordfish, bigeye tuna and tilefish from the Gulf of Mexico. Note that Atlantic mackerel is a Best Choice and king mackerel is not, so read the label. The FDA/EPA chart is worth a look if you eat fish often.

Before you add fish oil or change your medicine: food first is the safest place to start. If you take a blood thinner such as warfarin, please ask your pharmacist before adding fish oil or any omega-3 supplement, because they can interact. If you take a statin, blood-pressure medicine or diabetes medicine, don't stop, skip or change any of it because you're eating more fish. Fish works alongside your medicine, never instead of it. Check with your doctor or pharmacist before you change your medicine or make a big change in how you eat.

When to Talk to Your Doctor

Talk to your doctor if you have heart disease or have had a heart attack or stroke, if your triglycerides came back high on your last blood test, or if you're wondering whether fish oil makes sense for you. Those are real questions with individual answers, and your doctor knows your full picture. Ask your pharmacist about any supplement before you start it, especially if you take a blood thinner. And if you've ever had swelling, hives or trouble breathing after eating fish, avoid it and get checked for a fish allergy.

Chest pain, pressure, or sudden shortness of breath is an emergency. Call 911 or your local emergency number.

The bottom line: pick salmon, sardines, trout, herring or Atlantic mackerel at least twice a week, keep the cooking simple, and let lean fish and shrimp be your protein meals rather than your omega-3 meals. That's a week you can actually keep.
