How to Order Carnivore at Almost Any Restaurant

2026-08-25 by Chloe Navarro

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only — not medical advice. Details

The short answer: order the plainest protein on the menu and stop there

If you want to know what to order at restaurants on carnivore, here it is in one line. Pick the simplest cut of meat, fish, or eggs on the menu, ask for it cooked in butter and salt with no sauce, and skip every side that isn't more meat. That's the whole strategy. Steak plain. Eggs and bacon. Grilled chicken with nothing on it. Shrimp with butter.

Almost every restaurant on earth can do this, because you're asking them to do less work, not more. You're not asking for a substitute or a special preparation. You're asking them to leave things off. Servers hear "no sauce, no sides" constantly, and it barely registers. The part that feels hard is the social part, not the ordering part, and we'll get to that too.

The one sentence that works almost everywhere

Memorize this and you can stop overthinking it: "I'll have the [protein], plain, cooked in butter if you can, and no sides please."

That's it. Nine seconds. No explanation, no diet talk, no apology.

If the server asks why, "I have some food sensitivities" is true enough and ends the conversation instantly. People will push back on a diet. Nobody pushes back on a sensitivity. Here's the thing, you don't owe anybody your medical history over appetizers.

Steakhouse: the easiest room you'll ever walk into

This is home turf. Order the steak, ask for it plain with butter, and say no to the compound butter with herbs in it if you're being strict. Some kitchens finish steaks with a sauce or a seasoning blend by default, so "just salt and butter, please" covers you.

Skip the bread basket ritual. You don't have to make a thing of it. Just don't reach for it. If it lands in front of you, slide it toward whoever's next to you and keep talking.

Steakhouses will also usually do a second protein instead of a side. A few shrimp, a couple of eggs, an extra small cut. Ask. The worst they say is no.

Diner or breakfast place: order breakfast, always

Diners are the second easiest room. Eggs and bacon exist at every single one, at every hour. Order eggs any style, bacon or sausage, and hold the toast and potatoes before they ask.

Two things to watch for. Scrambled eggs sometimes get pancake batter or milk mixed in, and eggs are often cooked on a griddle coated in seed oil. If you care about either, ask for your eggs fried in butter. Most short-order cooks can do that without blinking.

Also, a lot of diners will happily do a plain burger patty or two with no bun. Ask for it "no bun, no dressing" and you'll get a perfectly good meal for eight dollars.

Mexican: fajitas without the tortillas

Fajitas are the move. It's meat cooked on a skillet, and it arrives separate from everything else, which means you're not picking things out of a pile. Order steak, chicken, or shrimp fajitas and say no to the tortillas, rice, and beans.

Carne asada is the other reliable one. It's usually just grilled beef with salt and lime. Skip the marinades that come with sugar or the sauces you can't identify, and don't assume the "grilled" chicken hasn't been sitting in something sweet.

Real talk: the chips hit the table before you've sat down. Same trick as the bread basket. Move them, don't announce it.

Italian: ask for the protein that lives under the pasta

Italian menus look scary and usually aren't. Almost every Italian restaurant has a grilled steak, a piece of fish, or chicken somewhere in the entrées section. Order that plain, no breading, no sauce, butter on the side.

What to skip is easy here. Anything breaded, anything "parmigiana," anything swimming in a sauce you didn't order. Cream sauces often have flour in them, and a lot of the oil in Italian kitchens isn't olive oil, whatever the menu says.

If the only plain option is chicken, take the chicken. A boring meal you can eat beats a great meal you regret.

Chinese and other Asian restaurants: this is the hard one

I'll be honest with you, this is the toughest category, and I'm not the only one who's given up mid-menu here. Almost everything is cooked in a sauce, and most sauces have sugar, soy, cornstarch, or all three.

Your options are usually steamed or plain-grilled. Ask for steamed shrimp, steamed chicken, or plain beef with no sauce and no marinade. Japanese places are friendlier, since sashimi is just fish and hibachi grills will cook a protein plain if you ask. Korean barbecue works too if you order unmarinated cuts.

If the kitchen can't do plain, eat before you go and order tea. That's a real strategy, not a failure.

The airport: eat before you fly, but here's the backup

Airports are expensive and thin on options, so eat before security if you can. If you can't, look for these in order: a sit-down restaurant with a burger you can order bunless, a breakfast spot with eggs and bacon, a sandwich counter that will sell you the meat without the bread.

Convenience stores in terminals usually carry hard-boiled eggs, beef sticks, and cheese if you eat dairy. Not glamorous. Perfectly fine for four hours.

I fly out of Vancouver a fair bit and my whole plan is a bag of beef sticks in my carry-on. It costs about four dollars and removes the entire problem.

What to skip, everywhere, without thinking about it

  • Sauces you didn't order. Most restaurant sauces are built on seed oils, sugar, or flour. "No sauce" is the single highest-value thing you can say.
  • The bread basket and the chip bowl. Not because one bite ruins you, but because the ritual is what gets people. Move it, don't fight it.
  • "Grilled" as a guarantee. Grilled often means marinated first. Ask.
  • Salad dressing. If you're eating a side salad at all, dressing is almost always the seed oil delivery system.
  • The cocktail you have out of habit. No judgment, just know it's there.

Handling the table without the five-minute speech

Here's what I've learned after a lot of awkward dinners. The less you explain, the less anybody cares. When you announce your diet, you've made it a topic, and now everyone at the table has an opinion and a cousin who tried it.

Order your steak, thank the server, and ask somebody a question about their kid. Conversation moves on in about eleven seconds.

If someone does press, keep it light and short. "I feel better eating this way" is unarguable. Nobody can debate how you feel. And if a family member keeps poking at it across three courses, that's about them, not your dinner.

One more thing worth knowing. Restaurant portions run big, and a lot of people eating out on carnivore either wildly overshoot or undershoot what they actually need. I'm not a doctor. I'm someone who's deep in this community and reads everything people are trying. If you're on medication or managing a diagnosed condition, talk to your healthcare provider before changing how you eat. Take the internet with a grain of salt, pun fully intended.

The takeaway

You are not "that person." You're someone who ordered a steak with no sauce. That's it. That's the whole imposition.

Pick the plainest protein, say your one sentence, skip the basket, and get back to the conversation. After about three dinners out it stops feeling like a performance and starts feeling like Tuesday.
