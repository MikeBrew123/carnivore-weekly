Tuna and Eggs Only: Does This Reset Actually Work?

2026-08-06 by Marcus Cole

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only — not medical advice. Details

The reset that won't die

Every few months it circles back. Tuna and eggs, nothing else, two weeks, "reset your metabolism." Somebody posts a before and after, the comments fill up, and 400 people start buying flats of canned tuna.

I get the appeal. Two ingredients. No cooking skill required. No decisions at 6pm when you're tired. Simplicity is the most underrated variable in nutrition, and this protocol is about as simple as it gets.

But simple isn't the same as smart. Let's run the numbers on this thing and then build a version that won't wreck you.

The mercury math

This is the part people wave off, and it's the part that actually matters.

The EPA reference dose for methylmercury is 0.1 micrograms per kilogram of bodyweight per day. If you weigh 180 pounds, that's about 8 micrograms a day. Call it 57 micrograms a week.

Canned light tuna, which is usually skipjack, averages around 0.126 parts per million mercury according to FDA testing. A drained 5 ounce can holds roughly 142 grams. Do the multiplication and you're looking at about 18 micrograms of mercury per can.

So a 180 pound guy hits his weekly ceiling at roughly three cans. Not three cans a day. Three cans a week.

Now look at what the reset actually asks for. Most versions have people eating four to six cans daily to hit protein targets. That's 70 to 110 micrograms a day, roughly ten times the reference dose, held for 14 straight days.

Albacore is worse. White albacore averages around 0.35 ppm, which puts a single 5 ounce can near 50 micrograms. One can a day already blows past the limit for most adults.

The reference dose has a safety buffer built in, so two weeks over it isn't a medical emergency for a healthy adult. Mercury also clears with a half-life of about 50 to 70 days, so it doesn't stay forever. But you're deliberately loading a neurotoxin to save money on ground beef. That's a bad trade.

The fat shortfall nobody talks about

Here's where the protocol falls apart even if you ignore mercury entirely.

Canned tuna in water is close to pure protein. A 5 ounce can gives you about 30 grams of protein, 1 gram of fat, and 130 calories. That's it. Eggs help, at 6 grams of protein and 5 grams of fat each, but they can't carry the whole load.

Run a typical day. Six cans of tuna plus twelve eggs gets you around 250 grams of protein, 66 grams of fat, and only 1,650 calories. Protein is supplying roughly 60 percent of your energy.

That's the zone where things go sideways. Your liver caps out on how fast it can process nitrogen from protein, somewhere around 35 to 40 percent of total calories for most people. Push past it and you get the classic symptoms. Nausea, headache, diarrhea, weakness, a weird persistent hunger that eating more lean protein makes worse.

Old timers called it rabbit starvation, because you can eat lean rabbit all day and still fall apart. Same mechanism.

Then there's the calorie hole. 1,650 calories isn't a reset, it's a crash diet wearing a costume. You'll lose weight in two weeks. Most of it is water and glycogen, and a chunk of it is muscle because you're under-fueled while still training.

What it actually costs

The budget argument doesn't hold either. People assume canned tuna is the cheap option. It isn't, not per calorie.

Price it per gram of protein and tuna looks fine, around 5 cents at $1.50 a can. Eggs run about 6 cents. Ground beef lands around 6 cents too. Nobody wins by much.

Now price it per 1,000 calories, which is what actually determines whether you can live on it.

  • Canned light tuna: roughly $11.50 per 1,000 calories
  • Eggs: roughly $5.20 per 1,000 calories
  • 80/20 ground beef: roughly $4.80 per 1,000 calories

Tuna is more than twice the cost of ground beef for the same energy. You're paying a premium for the privilege of eating something too lean to live on. That's the opposite of a budget protocol.

The mineral gap

One more thing nobody checks. Zinc.

A can of tuna carries under a milligram. An egg has about half a milligram. Stack a full day of tuna and eggs and you might scrape 12 milligrams, but a lot of that comes with competing absorption. Compare that to 100 grams of beef, which delivers around 5 milligrams on its own.

Zinc runs testosterone production, immune function, and wound healing. Two weeks of a shortfall won't hurt a healthy adult. But if you're training hard, you'll feel the recovery slow down, and you'll blame it on the wrong thing.

Who should skip this entirely

No hedging here. Don't run any version of this if you're pregnant, trying to get pregnant, or nursing. Fetal and infant brains are the specific reason mercury limits exist. FDA guidance is clear on this and it isn't a suggestion.

Same goes for kids, anyone with kidney disease, and anyone with a history of gout who hasn't cleared a high-purine load with their doctor.

The version I'd actually run

You want the simplicity. Fine. Keep the simplicity, fix the math.

Here's the protocol. Fourteen days.

  • Tuna: skipjack light only, maximum 3 cans per week. Never albacore on a daily schedule. This is a flavor rotation, not your protein base.
  • Eggs: 6 to 8 per day, cooked in butter or tallow. The cooking fat isn't optional, it adds 12 to 15 grams of fat you can't get from the eggs alone.
  • Fatty anchor: 8 to 10 ounces of 80/20 ground beef or fatty salmon on every day you're not eating tuna. This is what makes the protocol survivable.
  • Fat target: at least 55 percent of calories. Rough rule, roughly 1 gram of fat for every gram of protein.
  • Calories: 2,000 minimum for most adults. If you're training, more.
  • Salt: 1 to 2 teaspoons a day, spread across meals.

That's still three ingredients. Still no recipes. Still costs less than what you're eating now. It just won't leave you weak and mercury-loaded on day 10.

Measure it or don't bother

A reset without data is just a diet you'll forget about by March.

Track four things daily. Morning bodyweight, energy on a 1 to 10 scale at 3pm, sleep hours, and one training number that doesn't lie. Pick a benchmark set, a running time, whatever you can repeat.

On day 14, compare. If bodyweight dropped and energy climbed and your training number held or improved, the simplicity worked for you. Keep a version of it.

If energy tanked and your training number fell off a cliff, you underfed. That's not a metabolism problem. That's an arithmetic problem, and it's fixable.

Two weeks is long enough to see a signal and short enough that you can't do real damage. Just don't confuse a two-week reset with a strategy. Whatever you do on day 15 matters more than anything you did on day 3.

I'm not a doctor. I've coached people and competed myself, so I know what works in practice. But I'm not your doctor. If you're pregnant, nursing, on medication, or managing a diagnosed condition, talk to someone qualified before changing how you eat. Mercury limits in particular exist for real reasons. Your mileage may vary.
