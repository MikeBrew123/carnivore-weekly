Hair Shedding on Carnivore: Why Month Three Is Worst

2026-08-21 by Sarah Whitfield

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only — not medical advice. Details

Yes, it almost always grows back

If you're three months into carnivore and finding hair in the shower drain, in your brush, on your pillow, I want to give you the short answer first. This is nearly always temporary. Most women see the shedding slow down within two to three months of when it started, and regrowth follows within six to nine months.

The name for it is telogen effluvium. It's a diffuse shed, meaning your hair thins all over rather than in patches, and it's one of the most common things that happens after any big metabolic change. Rapid weight loss triggers it. Surgery triggers it. So does a fever, a death in the family, or stopping birth control. Your hair follicles aren't dying. They've stepped out of the growing phase early and gone to rest, and they'll step back in.

Why month three, and not month one

This is the part that confuses people, and it's worth understanding because it changes how you read the timing.

Your hair grows in cycles. At any moment, roughly 85 to 90 percent of your follicles are in the growing phase, called anagen. The rest are in the resting phase, telogen, where the hair sits in the follicle for about two to three months before it releases and falls out.

When your body goes through a sudden shift, and a sharp calorie drop counts as one, a chunk of those growing follicles get pushed into resting early. But they don't shed that day. They sit there for two to three months first. So the shedding you're seeing in month three is the echo of what happened in week one or two.

That delay matters. It means the trigger has usually already passed by the time you notice the problem. Your body isn't currently in crisis. It's finishing a process that started when you cut your intake in half overnight.

The protein floor

Hair is almost entirely keratin, a structural protein. When protein intake dips below what your body needs, hair is one of the first non-essential systems to get deprioritized. Your body doesn't care about your ponytail. It cares about your heart and your immune system.

Here's where carnivore gets a little counterintuitive. Most people assume an all-meat diet means plenty of protein by definition. But a lot of women in their fifties and sixties, especially those eating mostly fatty cuts and following high-fat advice, land well under what they need. Ribeye is delicious. It's also close to a one-to-one fat-to-protein ratio by calories, and if you're eating small portions because your appetite crashed, the total can be surprisingly low.

A reasonable floor for most women in this age range is somewhere around 1.2 to 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of goal body weight. For a woman aiming for 145 pounds, that's roughly 80 to 105 grams a day. If you've never actually counted, it's worth doing for three days just to see where you land. If you're low, leaner cuts help. Sirloin, chicken thighs, canned fish, eggs, and lean ground beef move the protein number up without adding much volume.

Iron and ferritin, the two numbers to actually check

This is the piece most people miss, and it's the one I'd push hardest on.

Ferritin is your stored iron. You can have a perfectly normal hemoglobin and a perfectly normal complete blood count while your ferritin is scraping bottom. Standard lab ranges often call anything above 12 or 15 ng/mL normal, but the research on hair loss suggests follicles want more than that. Several studies have found associations between low ferritin and diffuse shedding in women, with a number of dermatologists targeting 40 to 70 ng/mL for hair health specifically. The evidence isn't unanimous, and it's largely observational, so treat those numbers as a working target rather than a settled fact.

I have some personal stake in this one. I was born with beta thalassemia minor, which means I've lived with low-grade anemia my whole life. Diet didn't fix it and never will. What it taught me is that "normal CBC" and "enough iron for your hair" are two entirely different questions, and you have to ask for the ferritin test specifically because it's not on a standard panel.

Ask your doctor for ferritin, a complete blood count, and TSH with free T4. Thyroid problems are common in women over 45, and hair shedding is a classic early sign. Getting those three back gives you real information instead of guesswork.

Research shows general patterns. Your body may respond differently based on your own health history, so use these numbers as a conversation starter with your doctor, not a self-diagnosis.

When it's pointing at something else

Telogen effluvium has a shape to it. It's diffuse, it peaks and then eases, and the hair that comes out has a small white bulb at the root. If what you're seeing doesn't match that description, it's worth a closer look.

  • Distinct bald patches, round and smooth, rather than overall thinning
  • Widening at the part line with no shedding, which points toward pattern hair loss instead
  • Redness, scaling, burning, or scarring on the scalp
  • Shedding that's still heavy past the eight or nine month mark
  • Hair loss alongside deep fatigue, cold intolerance, or unexplained weight change

Any of those deserve a doctor's eyes, ideally a dermatologist's.

One more thing worth naming clearly. A number of common medications list hair shedding as a side effect, including some blood pressure drugs, statins, antidepressants, and thyroid medication when the dose isn't dialed in. If you're taking medications or have been diagnosed with any medical condition, you need individualized medical oversight. Don't make changes without consulting your doctor.

What to do while you wait

Hit your protein number consistently. Slow your rate of weight loss if it's been aggressive, since a gentler deficit is less of a shock to the system. Get your ferritin and thyroid checked. Be gentle with your hair, so less heat, less tension, looser ties.

And then be patient, which is the hardest part. Regrowth is slow and quiet. You'll see short new hairs standing up along your hairline before you see any real thickness, and those little flyaways are the good sign. Most women I've talked to say the shedding stopped bothering them a full two months before they could see the difference in the mirror.

I'm not a doctor. I've researched this deeply and worked with many people, but I'm not your doctor. If you have health conditions, take medications, or need specific guidance, talk to someone who knows your full medical picture. Everything I write is educational based on research and what I've seen work. Your situation might be different.
