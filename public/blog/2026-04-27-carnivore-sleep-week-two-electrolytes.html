Why Carnivore Wrecks Your Sleep in Week Two

2026-04-27 by Sarah Whitfield

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only, not medical advice. Details

You made it through week one feeling pretty good. Maybe a little tired, but you expected that. Then week two hits and suddenly you can't sleep. You're lying awake at 2 AM with your heart racing, legs twitching, and a vague sense of anxiety you can't explain. So you start wondering if carnivore is actually wrong for you.

It's not. Here's what's actually happening.

The Real Reason Sleep Falls Apart Around Day 10

When you cut carbohydrates down to near zero, your kidneys shift into a different gear. Insulin drops, and your kidneys respond by excreting sodium at a much faster rate than they used to. Sodium pulls water with it, which is why early carnivore comes with rapid weight loss. That's mostly water and glycogen leaving the body.

The problem is that sodium loss drags other electrolytes with it, specifically magnesium and potassium. By week two, you've been running a quiet deficit for ten days and your body is starting to feel it. Sleep is one of the first things to go.

Magnesium is the one that usually breaks sleep. Your nervous system uses it to calm down at night. When levels drop, you get exactly what most people describe in week two: racing thoughts, leg cramps, light and restless sleep, and that strange anxious feeling that shows up around bedtime for no obvious reason.

What Week Two Sleep Disruption Usually Looks Like

There's a recognisable pattern here, and knowing it helps, as long as we're honest that a pattern is not a diagnosis. If your sleep in week two looks like this, the mineral shift is a reasonable first thing to think about:

  • You fall asleep fine but wake up between 1 and 4 AM and can't get back to sleep
  • Your legs feel restless or crampy at night
  • You feel anxious or wired at bedtime even though you're tired
  • Headaches, especially in the afternoon or evening
  • Muscle twitching anywhere, not just the legs

If several of those are familiar and this is your second or third week, food and seasoning are a sensible place to start, and for a lot of people it does settle within a week or two.

One symptom I've deliberately taken out of that list. A heart that beats noticeably fast, or feels like it's skipping, does not belong with restless legs and afternoon headaches. Both too little and too much potassium can cause that feeling, and you cannot tell which one you're dealing with from the way it feels, so it isn't a symptom to answer by adding minerals. Ring your doctor and tell them you've recently changed how you eat. If it comes with chest pain, breathlessness, fainting or a heartbeat that won't settle, that's urgent care today rather than a nutrition question.

What to Actually Do About It

The part that comes before the practical part. Sodium and potassium are the two minerals where the right answer depends on things this page cannot see. If you take a daily pill for your blood pressure or your heart, a water pill, or anything to do with your kidneys, ask your doctor or your pharmacist before you increase either one, and that includes salt substitutes like Lite Salt or No Salt and any electrolyte packet. Some of those medicines already hold potassium in your body, so extra on top is the problem rather than the help. Have the same conversation if a blood test has ever come back with a note about your sodium, your potassium or your kidney function, or if anyone has ever told you to watch your salt. If you're honestly not sure whether that's you, that's a reason to ask rather than to assume it isn't.

For everyone else, here's what I'd actually do, and you'll notice I'm not giving you milligrams. The readers most likely to be harmed by a number are the least likely to know the number applies to them.

Sodium first, and by taste. A lot of people eating plain meat in week two are eating food they'd have called badly under-seasoned a month ago. Salt what you eat until it tastes right to you, and let your taste climb, because it usually does in the first fortnight. A cup of salted broth in the afternoon is a pleasant habit and it's food rather than a dose. What I've taken out of this section is the measured glass of salt water, because a stranger's sodium measured in teaspoons is the single thing here that most needs a clinician's eyes.

Magnesium is the sleep mineral. Magnesium glycinate is the form I'd look for. It's gentle on digestion and reaches the nervous system better than magnesium oxide, which is cheap but mostly ends up in your gut rather than your cells. How much is right for you depends on what else you take, so ask your pharmacist and buy the smallest bottle first. Take it in the evening. People who get on with it usually notice something within a few nights, and if you notice nothing after a couple of weeks then it wasn't the lever for you.

Potassium goes on the plate, not in a glass. Meat carries a reasonable amount, so if you're eating beef, lamb or pork with some variety you're probably in decent shape here. Two pounds of varied animal food a day, with liver about once a week, does more than any pill will. I'm not going to hand you a potassium supplement for cramps, and I want to say why rather than just leave it out: cramping does not tell you which direction your potassium has moved, the 99 mg on a supplement label is a serving size the industry picked rather than a dose measured against you, and if you take an ACE inhibitor, an ARB or a potassium-sparing diuretic then extra potassium is the risk rather than the remedy. Food is both the safer route and the one that actually works.

Timing Makes a Difference

When you take these matters, especially for sleep. Magnesium works best taken 60 to 90 minutes before bed, not with dinner. Taking it earlier means some of the calming effect has worn off by the time you actually lie down.

Sodium sits better spread across the day than loaded into one meal, and a large amount late can nudge blood pressure up temporarily in some people. Season breakfast and lunch the way you like them and let dinner be lighter on salt.

If you wake at 2 AM and can't get back to sleep, the honest answer is that this is a bad moment to be dosing anything. Get up, keep the lights low, have a glass of water, and go back to bed when you feel sleepy rather than when you feel fixed. A middle-of-the-night supplement on the nightstand turns a rough fortnight into a habit, and it's the version of this advice I'd least want a reader on heart medication to adopt at 2 AM without anyone watching.

How Long Does This Last?

If you address the electrolytes directly, most people see real improvement within three to five days. Week three and four are noticeably better for sleep than week two, even without the supplementation, because your kidneys gradually recalibrate to the new diet. But there's no reason to white-knuckle through two weeks of bad sleep when the fix is a few dollars of magnesium and more salt on your steak.

Some people need to keep supplementing magnesium long term, especially women with a history of anxiety or PCOS. If your sleep doesn't improve after ten days of consistent sodium and magnesium supplementation, that's worth exploring further with your doctor because there may be something else going on.

The Bigger Picture

Week two sleep disruption is one of the most common reasons people quit carnivore thinking it doesn't work for them. They're misreading a solvable electrolyte problem as a fundamental incompatibility with the diet. It's not. It's your kidneys doing exactly what they're supposed to do on a low-carb diet, and your supplementation just needs to catch up.

The diet isn't the problem. The transition is. And transitions have solutions.

I'm not a doctor. I've researched this deeply and worked with many people through carnivore adaptation, but I'm not your doctor. If you take medication for your blood pressure, your heart or your kidneys, or a water pill, or you've been told to watch your salt, the person who has your chart should be setting sodium and potassium rather than me. And if sleep hasn't improved after a couple of weeks of eating and salting normally, that's worth looking into properly rather than adding to. Everything here is educational. Your situation might be different.
