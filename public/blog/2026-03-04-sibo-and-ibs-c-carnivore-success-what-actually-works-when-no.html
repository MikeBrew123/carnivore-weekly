Carnivore Diet for IBS and SIBO: Why It Can Backfire

2026-03-04 by Sarah Whitfield

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only, not medical advice. Details

Carnivore can calm IBS symptoms for some people, because it removes most of the fermentable carbs that cause gas and bloating. But if you have IBS-C or SIBO, it can also make constipation and bloating worse at first, and the research on carnivore for IBS is still mostly personal reports, not trials. If you try it, go slower than standard carnivore advice, and do it with your gastroenterologist in the loop.

"Does the carnivore diet help with IBS?" is one of the questions I see most in comments, usually right next to "Can carnivore work with gout, IBS, or a bad gallbladder?" Here's my honest answer, and what I'd change if you have a sensitive gut.

If you've been diagnosed with IBS, SIBO or any gut condition, or you take medication for it, you need individualized medical oversight. Don't change your treatment plan without talking to your doctor.

Why carnivore helps some people with IBS

The best-studied diet for IBS is low-FODMAP. FODMAPs are certain carbs found in foods like onions, garlic, beans, wheat and some fruits. Gut bacteria ferment them, and that fermentation causes gas, bloating and pain in sensitive people. Low-FODMAP diets help a good share of people with IBS in clinical studies.

Plain meat, fish and eggs contain almost no FODMAPs. So carnivore is, by default, an extremely low-FODMAP way of eating. That's the most likely reason some people with IBS feel better on it. Fewer fermentable carbs, less gas, less bloating.

The research shows general patterns, though, and your gut may respond differently. There are no good trials of carnivore specifically for IBS or SIBO yet.

Why it can backfire with IBS-C or SIBO

Here's what I see in reader messages: "I cut out every plant, and I'm more constipated and more bloated than before." That's real, and it usually comes from a few things:

  • A sudden drop in food volume. Meat leaves less bulk behind. If your gut already moves slowly, less bulk can mean fewer, harder bowel movements at first.
  • Too much fat, too fast. Big fatty meals can sit heavy and cause bloating or loose stools while your digestion adjusts. Fat is not the enemy, but a sensitive gut needs time.
  • Big meals. One or two huge plates a day is common on carnivore. A gut with slow movement often handles smaller meals better.
  • Not enough fluid and salt. Early carnivore flushes water and salt, and being low on both makes constipation worse.

If any of that sounds like you, my posts on carnivore constipation and still being bloated two months in go deeper.

What I'd change if you have IBS-C or SIBO

Here's what I've seen work, as a starting point to discuss with your doctor:

  1. Start with softer, easier meats. Ground beef, slow-cooked chuck, fish and eggs are gentler than big, tough steaks. Chew well. It sounds too simple, but it matters.
  2. Eat smaller meals. Three moderate meals often sit better than one or two large ones, at least for the first month.
  3. Bring fat up gradually. Start with leaner cuts and add butter or tallow a little at a time. Your stools will tell you a lot. Firm and hard can mean too little fat; loose and urgent can mean too much.
  4. Drink water between meals and salt your food to taste. If you take blood pressure medication or have heart or kidney concerns, ask your doctor about salt first.
  5. Try bone broth. Plenty of readers with sensitive guts find warm broth soothing and easy, especially early on. My post on bone broth when your digestion is wrecked has the details.
  6. Walk after meals. A gentle walk helps your gut keep things moving. Even ten minutes after dinner counts.
  7. Change one thing at a time. If dairy or eggs are in the mix, take one out for a week and watch what happens. Change three things at once and you'll never know which one mattered.

If you have SIBO, ask your gastroenterologist how diet fits with any treatment they recommend. Diet alone may not be the whole answer, and that's a question for someone who can test you.

See a doctor first if any of these apply

IBS is diagnosed by ruling other things out. Before you try any elimination diet, get checked if you have:

  • Blood in your stool, or black, tarry stools
  • Weight loss you can't explain
  • New bowel changes after age 50
  • Pain that wakes you up at night
  • A family history of colon cancer, celiac disease or inflammatory bowel disease
  • Ongoing vomiting, fever or anemia

These need a proper workup, and sometimes a scope, before anyone calls it IBS. An elimination diet can hide symptoms without fixing the cause.

The bottom line

Carnivore is a very low-FODMAP diet, and that's why some people with IBS feel better on it. With IBS-C or SIBO it often needs a slower, gentler start: softer meats, smaller meals, fat added gradually, and plenty of fluid and movement. If it makes your symptoms worse after a few weeks of careful changes, that's useful information, not a failure. Take it back to your doctor.

I'm not a doctor. I've researched this deeply and worked with a lot of people, but I'm not your doctor. IBS and SIBO both need a proper diagnosis, and their symptoms overlap with conditions you don't want to manage with diet alone, like celiac disease and inflammatory bowel disease. Try any change like this alongside your gastroenterologist. Everything here is educational. Your situation might be different.

-Sarah
