Losing Weight but Getting Weaker After 60?

2026-11-24 by Sarah Whitfield

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only, not medical advice. Details

The scale is finally moving, your clothes are looser, and yet the stairs feel steeper than they did in spring. If you're over 60 and losing weight on carnivore, that mix is worth paying attention to. Some of what you're losing may be muscle, not just fat.

The good news is that losing muscle on a carnivore diet isn't a given. It usually comes down to three things you can change: how much protein lands in each meal, how fast the weight is coming off, and whether your muscles get asked to do any work. If you want a starting point for your own numbers, the free macro calculator works from your goal weight. Below are the everyday signs, simple home checks, and the fixes that protect strength while the scale drops.

The everyday signs that weight loss is costing muscle

Muscle loss rarely announces itself. It shows up in small moments you might write off as "just getting older."

  • Stairs feel harder, even though you're carrying less weight up them.
  • Jar lids win more often. You hand the pickle jar to someone else, or reach for a rubber gripper.
  • You need your hands to get out of a chair. Pushing off the armrests used to be optional.
  • Your walking has slowed. Friends drift ahead on the trail, or the walk to the mailbox takes longer.
  • Clothes are looser but your arms feel softer, not firmer. Less size with less tone is a clue that some of the loss is muscle.

One sign alone might mean nothing. Two or three while you're losing weight is a pattern worth acting on.

Why this happens more after 60

Older muscle responds less to protein. Researchers call it anabolic resistance. A meal that fed your muscles well at 35 may not fully "switch on" repair at 65, so one big steak at dinner can leave them underfed most of the day.

Add a calorie deficit on top, and your body has every reason to give up some muscle along with the fat. Fast weight loss makes that trade worse.

Simple home checks: track it for yourself

These aren't tests that diagnose anything. They're a way to see your own trend, the same way you'd track the scale. Write the results down and repeat them monthly.

  • Five chair stands. Sit in a sturdy kitchen chair with no wheels, arms crossed over your chest. Stand up fully and sit back down five times, as fast as feels safe. Time it. Use the same chair each month.
  • The jar check. Pick one jar you open often. Notice whether it's easy, a struggle, or a hand-off. Grip tends to track overall strength.
  • The mailbox walk. Time your normal-pace walk to the mailbox, the end of the block, or a set distance in a hallway. Same route, same shoes.

If your chair-stand time is creeping up month after month while the scale drops, that's useful information. Bring the numbers to your next appointment. It starts a far better conversation than "I just feel weaker."

Diet fixes that protect strength

This isn't a protein-math post. For the full number breakdown, see How Much Protein After 50 to Keep Your Muscle.

Total protein. Most people on carnivore land around 0.8 to 1.0 g of protein per pound of goal body weight. This is not for everyone. Talk to your doctor first if you have high blood pressure, kidney or heart problems, diabetes, are pregnant, or take medicine.

Protein per meal. After 60, this is the part people miss. Aim to get 30 to 40 g into each meal rather than saving it all for dinner. In real food, that's roughly 5 to 6 oz of cooked steak or ground beef, or four eggs with a small beef patty on the side.

Pace. Over 50, a loss of 0.5 to 1 lb a week after the first water week is a good pace. Losing faster costs more muscle. If you're dropping well beyond that and feeling weaker, eating a little more, starting with protein, is often the fix. When you trim, trim added butter and tallow first, never the meat.

Don't under-eat because you're not hungry. Lower appetite is common on carnivore. If you're skipping meals simply because you forgot to be hungry, your muscles pay for it.

Movement that counts, no gym required

Protein gives muscle the building blocks. Work tells it to keep them. No gym needed, just a few minutes most days.

  • Chair stands. The same move as your home check. A few sets of five to ten during the day.
  • Wall push-ups. Hands on the wall at shoulder height, step back, bend your elbows, push away. Move closer to the counter as they get easy.
  • Carrying groceries. Carry the bags in yourself, one in each hand, standing tall. It's grip, shoulders and legs all at once.
  • Resistance bands. Cheap and light, good for rows and curls during the news.

Start easy. Finish feeling like you could have done a little more.

An honest note on the research

A 2026 study in Frontiers in Nutrition looked at protein patterns and sarcopenic obesity in middle-aged and older adults, and found plant protein, not animal protein, linked with lower risk. That's worth taking seriously. It's observational, so it can't show cause. What trials consistently support is enough total protein plus resistance work, and that's what this post is built on.

When weakness needs care, not a home check

See a doctor promptly, or get emergency help, if you notice any of these:

  • Sudden weakness on one side of your body, a drooping face, or slurred speech. Call emergency services.
  • Weakness along with weight loss you're not trying for.
  • Any fall, or feeling like you're about to fall more often.
  • Muscle pain with dark, tea-colored urine.
  • New weakness or muscle aches after starting a medicine, statins included. Tell your prescriber. Any change to your medicine is your prescriber's decision.

If you're also feeling lightheaded when you get up, that's a related but different problem, and I covered it in Dizzy When You Stand Up on Carnivore After 60.

The bottom line

Getting lighter should make life easier. If the stairs, the jars or the chair say otherwise, listen. Spread your protein across the day, slow the pace if it's racing, and give your muscles a little work. Then time those chair stands again next month.

I'm not a doctor. I've researched this deeply, but I'm not your doctor. Everything I write is educational, based on research and what I've seen work. Your situation might be different.
