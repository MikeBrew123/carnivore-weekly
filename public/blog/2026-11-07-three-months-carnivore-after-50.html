3 Months of Carnivore After 50: What Should Have Changed

2026-11-07 by Sarah Whitfield

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only, not medical advice. Details

Ninety days is the point where most women over 50 stop asking "am I doing this right?" and start asking "is this working?" That's a fair question, and three months is a fair time to ask it. By now, most people on carnivore have lost the early water weight plus roughly 6-12 pounds of fat at the pace that's typical over 50, their waist has come in, their afternoon energy has steadied, and they think about food a lot less.

What often hasn't changed yet: the stubborn belly, the last few sleep wrinkles, and sometimes the scale itself, even while your clothes fit differently. That's normal. Here's the check I'd walk a friend through.

The scale: slower than you hoped, probably right on pace

Week one usually brings a quick drop of 3-7 pounds, less for smaller and older bodies. That's mostly water and stored carbohydrate. After that, a good pace over 50 is 0.5-1 pound a week. Not dramatic. Real. Our carnivore weight loss timeline walks through the first two months week by week.

A lot of you tell me the scale went quiet around weeks six to ten, then dropped again. That stop-and-start pattern is common. Look at the 12-week line, not the last 7 days.

If you never set your numbers, or your weight has changed enough that they're out of date, the free macro calculator is a decent starting point.

The waist: the number I'd trust more

If you only do one thing this week, get out a soft tape measure. Measure at your belly button, standing relaxed, first thing in the morning. Many women lose an inch or two off the waist in three months while the scale moves less than they'd like.

Why the gap? Higher protein can help you hold on to muscle while fat comes off, and muscle is dense. I lost my own weight steadily rather than dramatically, and what I noticed first wasn't a number. It was a pair of pants I'd stopped wearing.

The very low belly is usually the last place to change after menopause. If that's where you're still soft, give it more time before deciding nothing's working.

Energy: steadier, with a few flat days

The roughest stretch for most people is days 3-10. Most feel adapted by weeks four to six, and full fat-adaptation can take 8-12 weeks. So at 90 days, you should be past the fog.

If you're still tired most days, salt is the first thing I'd look at. Lower insulin makes your kidneys let go of more sodium and water, so most people on carnivore land around 3-5 g of sodium a day (about 1.5-2 teaspoons of salt), spread through the day. One teaspoon of salt is about 2.3 g of sodium. This is not for everyone. Talk to your doctor first if you have high blood pressure, kidney or heart problems, diabetes, are pregnant, or take medicine.

The other common culprit is eating too little. Many women over 50 drift toward one small meal a day. Most do better with 30-40 g of protein per meal, two or three meals a day.

Sleep: better, but not always fixed

Sleep often gets worse in weeks two and three, then improves. By month three, many women report fewer 3 am wake-ups and fewer night sweats. Not all, though. Menopause doesn't stop because you changed your plate.

If sleep is still rough, cut coffee after noon, check your salt (a small salty drink before bed helps some people), and ask your pharmacist about magnesium glycinate in the evening. Skip magnesium oxide, since it absorbs poorly. Your pharmacist is the right person for the amount, because your kidneys clear magnesium and some medicines change what's safe.

Hunger: the quietest, biggest change

This is the one people don't expect. By three months, most readers say they've stopped thinking about food between meals. For me, that was the real change: not thinking about food all afternoon.

If you're still white-knuckling cravings, look at protein first, then fat. Protein is the anchor and fat is the lever. Around 1:1 fat to protein by grams is where most people feel full. Too lean usually means hungry.

What often hasn't changed yet

  • Your labs. Ninety days is a good time to recheck, not a time to expect perfect numbers. I've covered what cholesterol usually does after 90 days and what tends to happen to your A1c in 90 days, so bring those questions to your doctor.
  • Hair. Some women notice extra shedding around month three. It often follows the early weight loss and usually settles.
  • Belly and hips. Often last. Patience here is part of the plan.

Signs it's time to change something

Here's what I've seen work. If your waist and the scale have both been flat for 3-4 weeks, that's a real stall, and there's an order to fixing it:

  • Confirm it with the tape measure, not just the scale.
  • Check sleep, stress and salt.
  • Write down everything you eat for 7 days, including butter, tallow, cheese and cream.
  • Recalculate your numbers at your current weight.
  • Then try 2 weeks without dairy, or a planned maintenance break.

If you're there now, stalled at month three walks through each step in detail.

See a doctor soon if...

  • You feel dizzy or lightheaded when you stand, especially if you take blood pressure medicine.
  • You have shaky, sweaty, confused spells and take diabetes medicine.
  • Your heart races or skips and it doesn't settle with salt and fluids.
  • You get sharp pain under your right ribs after meals.
  • Hair loss keeps getting worse after month four, or you feel cold and exhausted all the time.
  • You're losing weight fast without trying, well beyond a pound or two a week.

Chest pain, fainting or trouble breathing means emergency care, not a blog post. And any change to your medicine is your prescriber's decision. Your doctor may need to adjust doses as your blood sugar and blood pressure change.

Your 90-day check, in one minute

Grab a pen. Waist smaller than day one? Energy steadier? Fewer cravings? Sleeping at least a little better? If you said yes to two or more, it's working, even if the scale is being stubborn. The version you can keep doing is the one that wins.

I'm not a doctor. I've read the research closely and listened to a lot of you, but I'm not your doctor. Everything here is education based on that reading and what readers tell me. Your situation might be different.
