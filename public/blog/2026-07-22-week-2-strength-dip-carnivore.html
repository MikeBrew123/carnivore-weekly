Carnivore Week 2 Strength Dip: Why It Happens and the Fix

2026-07-22 by Marcus Cole

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only — not medical advice. Details

Your numbers are going to drop. Here's why that's normal.

You start carnivore. Week one feels okay. Then week two hits and your bench moves like it's bolted to the floor. Your last set of squats feels like your first. Your sprints are flat.

Stop panicking. This is expected. It's not a sign carnivore "ruins performance." It's a fuel-switching problem, and it has a timeline. Let me walk you through what's happening and exactly when your strength comes back.

What's actually happening in week 2

For years you ran on carbs. Your muscles store carbs as glycogen. That's your fast fuel for anything explosive. Heavy singles, jumps, sprints, a hard grappling scramble. All of it taps glycogen first.

When you cut carbs to near zero, those glycogen tanks run low. Your body hasn't finished building the machinery to run on fat and ketones yet. So you're stuck in the gap. Low on the old fuel, not efficient on the new one. That gap is where your gym numbers dip.

Here's the key detail most people miss. Explosive lifts suffer first and worst. Your one-rep maxes, your speed work, your power output. Those lean hardest on glycogen. Slower, grindy strength holds up better. Steady-state cardio holds up best of all once you're adapted.

So if your five-mile jog feels fine but your box jumps feel dead, that's not random. That's the physiology doing exactly what it does.

The timeline: when it dips and when it comes back

Nobody can hand you an exact date. Bodies vary. But the general pattern is consistent enough to plan around.

  • Week 1: You might feel fine or slightly off. Water and glycogen are draining.
  • Weeks 2 to 3: The dip. Strength and power feel noticeably down. This is the hardest stretch.
  • Weeks 4 to 6: Fat-adaptation kicks in. Energy stabilizes. Numbers start climbing back toward baseline.
  • Weeks 6 to 12: Most people are back to their old strength or close. Endurance often feels better than before.

That's the honest range. A 2 to 6 week dip, then a rebound. If you quit in week 3, you quit right before the payoff. Don't be that guy.

One thing worth knowing. Peak explosive power for some athletes stays slightly below their old carb-fueled ceiling. If you're a competitive sprinter chasing hundredths of a second, that matters. If you're a normal person lifting to stay strong and lean, you won't notice it once you're adapted. Most readers here fall in the second group.

How to train through the dip

You don't stop training. You adjust. Here's the protocol.

1. Cut your volume by 30 to 50 percent for two to three weeks. This is the big one. Don't try to hit personal records during adaptation. Drop your sets, keep the movements, lower the intensity. You're maintaining, not building. Think of it as a planned deload that happens to line up with your diet switch.

2. Salt before you train. When you cut carbs, your kidneys dump sodium and water fast. Low sodium equals weak, dizzy, flat workouts. Add roughly half a teaspoon of salt to water 30 to 60 minutes pre-session. Add potassium and magnesium through food or supplements across the day. This alone fixes a huge chunk of "carnivore made me weak" complaints. The weakness was often just an electrolyte crash.

3. Hit your protein target every single day. Aim for roughly 0.8 to 1 gram of protein per pound of goal bodyweight. For a lot of readers that's 120 to 160 grams a day. Protein protects your muscle while your fuel system rebuilds. Miss it and you'll lose strength that has nothing to do with adaptation. If you want a personalized number, the free macro calculator on the site will run it for your stats.

4. Eat enough fat. This is your new fuel. If you go low-carb AND low-fat by accident, you're just starving. That's not a diet, that's a hole. Fatty cuts, eggs, butter. Let your energy come from fat since it's no longer coming from carbs.

5. Sleep like it's part of the program. Because it is. Seven to nine hours. Adaptation is a recovery process. Short sleep slows the whole thing down and makes the dip feel worse than it is.

If you train fasted or eat once a day, the fuel timing matters even more during this window. I broke down the fueling side of that in this piece on running long distances on one meal a day. Same principle applies to lifting. Get the fuel in, then work.

Adaptation or under-eating? How to tell

This is where people get it wrong. They blame "adaptation" when they're actually just not eating enough. Different problems, different fixes. Here's how to sort it out.

It's probably normal adaptation if:

  • You're inside that 2 to 6 week window
  • Strength is down but you're not ravenous or dizzy all day
  • Slow strength holds up better than explosive work
  • Your salt and protein are dialed in
  • Energy is slowly trending better week over week

It's probably under-eating if:

  • You're hungry constantly, cold, and cranky
  • The weakness is getting worse past week 6, not better
  • You're skipping meals or eating tiny portions to "speed fat loss"
  • You're low on both carbs and fat
  • You feel worse after months, not just weeks

The fix for under-eating is simple. Eat more food. More fat, more protein, more total calories for a couple of weeks. If your numbers recover fast once you eat, it was fuel, not physiology.

And a reminder. You don't have to be an athlete to go through this. Even people who barely train hit the same fuel-switch fog. Sarah covered the non-training side in her piece on adapting to keto without exercise. The metabolic story is the same. Training just makes the dip louder because you're asking your body for output while it rewires.

The bottom line

A drop in your gym numbers during week 2 isn't failure. It's a phase with a start and an end. Cut volume, salt up, hit protein, sleep, and give it 4 to 6 weeks.

Track it so you can see the rebound instead of guessing. Log your top set weight and how it felt, once a week. When the line turns back up, and it will, you'll have proof instead of hope. That's the whole game. Measure it, adjust, keep showing up.

I'm not a doctor. I've coached people and competed myself, so I know what works. But I'm not your doctor. If you have health issues or take meds, check with someone qualified before big diet changes. Everything here is based on what works in practice and what research supports. Your mileage may vary.
