Carnivore Breakfast Without Eggs: 7 Easy Options

2026-11-17 by Marcus Cole

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only, not medical advice. Details

You don't need eggs for a carnivore breakfast. Ground beef patties, leftover roast, canned salmon, sardines, chicken thighs, homemade pork sausage and a quick steak all do the job. Each one below gets you to 30-40 g of protein with a cooking time of 15 minutes or less, or a reheat of about 3.

Maybe eggs don't agree with you. Maybe you're just sick of them. Or maybe egg prices have been jumping around again and a dozen doesn't stretch like it used to. Whatever the reason, the fix is the same: put a real piece of meat on the plate first thing.

The target: 30-40 g of protein at breakfast

Here's the number that matters. Most people on carnivore aim for 30-40 g of protein per meal, and that matters more after 50, when your body gets less out of small, scattered doses. Most also land around 3-5 g of sodium a day, spread across meals, so salt your breakfast properly. This is not for everyone. Talk to your doctor first if you have high blood pressure, kidney or heart problems, diabetes, are pregnant, or take medicine.

If you want a starting point for your daily numbers, the free macro calculator works it out from your goal weight in about two minutes. For the longer explanation of protein targets, see how much protein you actually need on carnivore.

One note on the math. Meat loses water as it cooks, so I give raw weights for fresh cooking and cooked weights for leftovers.

1. Ground beef patties

Protein: 6 oz raw 80/20 ground beef gives about 29 g. Go to 8 oz raw and you're near 39 g. Time: 8-10 minutes in a hot pan.

The cheapest, most forgiving option here. Press two thin patties, salt both sides, and cook 4 minutes a side. Cook a full pound at once and you've got breakfast for two or three days.

If you eat liver, grate 2 oz into each pound before you shape the patties. Two pounds a week gives you 4 oz of liver, the low end of the 4-8 oz per week cap we use. Don't go past that.

2. Leftover chuck roast

Protein: 4 oz cooked chuck is roughly 30-34 g. Time: 3-4 minutes to reheat.

This is the one I push hardest on people cooking for one or two. My Saturday routine is a chuck roast in the slow cooker for about 8 hours on low. It feeds dinner that night and breakfast for days after. Shred it, pack 4 oz portions, and freeze half if you live alone.

3. Canned salmon

Protein: 5 oz drained canned salmon is about 33 g. Time: 2 minutes, no stove needed.

Open, drain, salt, eat. Or shape it into small patties and fry 3 minutes a side. My mother is 82 and lives alone, and canned salmon is one of the few proteins she'll reliably eat when nobody's there to cook. That's the whole case for it.

4. Sardines

Protein: one 3.75 oz can is about 22-23 g. Two cans gets you to roughly 45 g, or pair one can with a 3 oz reheated patty. Time: 1 minute.

Buy them packed in water or olive oil, not in sauce. Read the label on anything flavored. They're cheap and keep for years in the cupboard.

5. Homemade pork sausage patties

Protein: 6 oz raw ground pork is about 28-29 g. Time: 10-12 minutes.

Store sausage often has sugar or fillers, so make your own. Mix a pound of ground pork with about 1 teaspoon of salt, shape into patties, and cook until there's no pink in the middle. Pork needs to be cooked through, so give it the full time.

Cook a pound, refrigerate what's left, and reheat for 2-3 minutes over the next few days.

6. Chicken thighs

Protein: 5 oz of cooked boneless thigh meat is about 35-36 g. Time: 20-22 minutes in an air fryer at 400°F, or reheated leftovers in 3 minutes.

Treat this as a batch item. Cook a tray of 6-8 thighs on Sunday. They reheat better than chicken breast and they're usually cheaper per pound. Skin on keeps you fuller; skin off trims fat.

7. A quick breakfast steak

Protein: 4 oz cooked sirloin is about 34 g. Time: 6-8 minutes for a thin cut.

Look for thin sirloin, flat iron, or minute steaks. A cut under an inch thick needs about 3 minutes a side in a hot pan. Cooking steak for dinner? Cook one extra for tomorrow.

The quick-reference list

  • Ground beef, 6-8 oz raw: 29-39 g protein, 8-10 min
  • Chuck roast, 4 oz cooked: 30-34 g, 3-4 min reheat
  • Canned salmon, 5 oz drained: about 33 g, 2 min
  • Sardines, 1-2 cans: 22-45 g, 1 min
  • Pork patties, 6 oz raw: 28-29 g, 10-12 min
  • Chicken thighs, 5 oz cooked: 35-36 g, 3 min reheat
  • Sirloin, 4 oz cooked: about 34 g, 6-8 min

Want a bit more? A cup of bone broth on the side adds a few grams of protein, often around 8-10 g, plus sodium. Store-bought broth can carry 800-1,200 mg of sodium per cup, so count it toward your salt for the day.

Dairy is optional

You don't need cheese or butter to make any of these work. Cook in the meat's own fat, or use tallow or lard. If dairy suits you, a pat of butter on the roast or a slice of cheese on a patty is fine. If you're stalled, it's one of the later things to look at, not the first.

The protocol for this week

  • Pick two options. One cook-fresh, one batch. Ground beef plus chuck roast is the easiest pair.
  • Cook once on the weekend. A pound of patties and a roast covers 5-7 breakfasts for one person.
  • Portion it. 4 oz cooked containers, labelled with the day you cooked them.
  • Keep a backup in the cupboard. Two cans of sardines or salmon for the morning nothing's ready.
  • Check in at day 7. Were you full until lunch? If not, add 2 oz of meat before you add anything else.

Stop overthinking it. Breakfast is meat, salt and a few minutes. If budget is the real reason you're off eggs, Chloe's guide to ground beef as the cheapest carnivore strategy runs the numbers.
