What to Say When They Ask About Your Plate

2026-09-12 by Chloe Navarro

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only, not medical advice. Details

Okay, so here's the short version, because you probably found this on a Tuesday with a work lunch you're already dreading. When someone comments on your plate, say something short and friendly, then ask them about themselves. "I'm keeping it simple lately, it's been going well. How was your weekend?" That's the whole move. You don't explain, you don't defend, and you definitely don't teach.

That works because nobody has ever changed their mind about food while holding a paper plate. You can't win a nutrition debate at a table. You can only end the topic or stretch it out, and short warm answers end it. Long answers with reasons in them are an invitation, and somebody will accept.

The trap nobody warns you about

Here's the thing. There's a real pull toward the cutting reply, and I felt it too the first time somebody made a face at my lunch.

I saw a thread recently where someone nearly got into it with coworkers over skipping the rice. It turned into more than a hundred comments of people workshopping zingers, sharper and sharper. Several were aimed at people's bodies and their blood sugar, which is rough to read when a lot of us are managing our own.

I'm not going to hand you any of those, because of what they cost. You land the perfect line, everybody goes quiet, and now you're the person at work who's weird about food. You won the exchange and lost the lunch.

Most of the time, it isn't an attack

"That's it? Just meat?" almost never means "you're wrong and I'd like to fight." Usually it's one of three things. They're curious. They're making conversation and food is what's in front of them. Or they feel self-conscious about their own plate, and talking about yours is easier.

Hearing curiosity as criticism is what starts most of these arguments. Answer a casual question with a defense and they'll wonder why you're so worked up, and then it really does become one.

Lines that close the topic instead of opening it

Keep them short and a little boring. Boring is your friend here.

  • "I'm keeping it simple these days."
  • "This is what's been sitting right with me lately."
  • "It's a health thing, honestly pretty dull."
  • "I'm the picky one today, ignore me."
  • "Long story, short version is it's working for me."
  • "Everybody's got a thing. This one's mine."
  • "Honestly? I just really like steak."
  • "I'm trying something for a few months, I'll know more later."

Then hand it back. "How's your daughter doing?" works on almost anyone. People would rather talk about themselves than about your dinner.

Different rooms, different answers

The work lunchroom. Shortest possible answer, because you'll see these people tomorrow. One line, a smile, a question back. If someone keeps pushing, "I'll spare you the whole thing" said cheerfully is a full sentence.

A potluck where someone cooked for you. This one's tender, because refusing food can feel like refusing the person. Lead with the compliment. "This looks amazing, can I get the recipe for my husband?" Take a small portion, take a plate home, or be visibly delighted by whatever meat is on the table. Some people find that bringing a dish that fits their plan solves it entirely, since now there's something they can eat.

Restaurants with friends. Order without narrating. Ask for the swap quietly, thank the server, move on. If ordering is the stressful part, we wrote up how to order carnivore at almost any restaurant without a whole production.

Family holidays. Hard, because everyone has history and everyone's tired. Bringing a dish helps. So does eating something small before you go, so you're not standing there hungry while people discuss your plate. There's more in our piece on doing carnivore socially with family.

Church and community meals. Food is how a lot of people say I love you, especially in a church basement. "I already ate, but I'd love a coffee and a seat next to you" keeps the relationship and skips the casserole.

The repeat offender

Then there's the one who brings it up every single time. Same joke, same tone, every family dinner.

Try this. Use the same flat, pleasant line every time, word for word. "Yep, still doing my thing." No new information, no energy, no reaction. People who do this are fishing for a response, and when the response is identical and boring twelve times running, most of them quietly stop.

If that doesn't work, have one calm private word. Not at the table, not in front of anyone. Something like, "I know you don't mean anything by it, but the food comments land harder than you'd think. Can we let that one go?" Most people are genuinely surprised, and most of them stop.

And if they don't, you're allowed to simply not engage. A "mm" and pass the potatoes. You aren't obligated to defend your lunch to the same person forever.

When they're worried, not critical

This one is different. When your husband or your adult daughter brings it up, it's usually fear wearing a critical face. They're not scoring points. They're scared something will happen to you.

Snapping back at worry makes it worse. That conversation goes better on the couch than at the table, and we have a whole post on what to do when your spouse thinks this diet will hurt you.

And when a family member asks "have you talked to your doctor about this?", that's a fair question, not an insult. Your doctor is on your team. "Yes, I've got labs coming up and we'll look at the numbers together" is a good answer and usually the end of it. Bloodwork belongs in an exam room anyway. Nobody has ever settled a lipid panel next to the deviled eggs.

You don't owe anyone your medical history

Real talk. You can be warm and still be private. Your labs, your weight, your diagnoses, none of that is required to attend a lunch. "It's a health thing" is a complete answer, and anyone who pushes past it is the one being rude.

Nobody remembers what you ate. They remember whether you were fun to sit with.

I'm not a doctor. I'm someone who's deep in this community and reads everything. I can tell you what people are trying and what tends to go well socially, but your health decisions belong with you and your doctor. If you take medication or you're managing a diagnosed condition, please make diet changes with your healthcare provider, not with an article.
