Food Noise Went Quiet and Nobody Warned You

2026-08-07 by Chloe Navarro

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only — not medical advice. Details

Okay so you've probably noticed the phrase creeping into your feed. "Food noise." Six months ago it lived almost entirely in Ozempic forums, where people described the constant background chatter about snacks finally going quiet. Now it's everywhere in carnivore and keto spaces, and half the posts using it aren't about medication at all.

Someone posts on day 40 of eating ribeyes and eggs: "The food noise is gone. Is that normal?" And the comments split into two camps. Half say congrats, welcome to the other side. The other half say something quieter, more careful. They say they felt the same way and it kind of freaked them out.

Real talk: that second group doesn't get enough airtime. So let's talk about it.

What people actually mean by food noise

The term came out of GLP-1 medication communities, where users kept describing the same thing in almost identical words. Not hunger. Not cravings exactly. More like a radio station in the back of your head that never turns off.

What's for dinner. Is there anything in the fridge. I should probably eat before that meeting. There's cake in the break room. Do I want the cake. I shouldn't want the cake. I'm going to think about that cake for four hours.

For a lot of people, that loop runs all day, every day, for decades. It's so constant that you stop noticing it, the way you stop hearing a fridge hum until it shuts off. Then it shuts off and the silence is loud.

Here's the thing. GLP-1 drugs didn't invent that experience. They just gave people a clean word for it. And once a word exists, everyone who ever felt the thing goes "oh, THAT'S what that was."

Why it showed up in carnivore forums

Because a lot of us have the same experience without the prescription. Not everyone. Not automatically. But it's common enough that it comes up almost weekly somewhere in the community.

The usual pattern people describe: first two weeks are rough, week three gets stable, and somewhere around week four or six they realize they forgot to eat lunch. Not white-knuckled through lunch. Forgot. The reminder that used to fire at 11:30 just didn't fire.

There's plausible reasoning for why a very high protein, very low carb way of eating might quiet that signal. Protein is filling. Blood sugar stops doing the rollercoaster thing. There's no snack decision to make when the food list has nine items on it.

I'd also point out the boring explanation nobody likes: when you remove most of the foods that were engineered to be thought about, you think about food less. Hyper-palatable stuff is designed to occupy mental real estate. Ground beef is not.

The part that catches people off guard

Everyone talks about food noise stopping like it's the finish line. Nobody warns you it can feel like a loss.

I've seen versions of this posted over and over. People say things like:

  • "I don't look forward to anything anymore."
  • "Food used to be my main hobby and now it's a chore."
  • "My whole family bonds over meals and I feel like I'm faking it."
  • "I'm eating because the clock says to, not because I want to."
  • "I should be happy about this. Why do I feel flat?"

That last one is the real one. There's guilt attached to not enjoying the thing you asked for.

Food isn't only fuel for most of us. It's reward, structure, comfort, the reason you call your mom on Sunday. If it was doing five jobs and it stops doing four of them, that's a real gap. It's not a character flaw to notice the gap.

I've had my own version. There was a stretch last winter here in Whistler where I genuinely couldn't tell you what I ate for three days running. Not because it was bad. Because it registered about as much as brushing my teeth. I remember thinking, huh, I used to have opinions about food. Where'd those go.

When quiet isn't the good kind

Here's where I'll stop being breezy about it.

The community sometimes treats "I don't think about food" as a scoreboard. Less noise equals more discipline equals winning. That framing is a problem, because there are a few different things that can look identical from the outside.

Genuine satiety looks like: you eat when you're hungry, you stop when you're done, you don't obsess in between, and you're eating enough.

Things worth a second look: dropping well below what you need without noticing, weight coming off faster than you planned, energy tanking, hair thinning, sleep falling apart, mood going flat across the board and not just around food. Also, if the quiet came with a new fear of eating rather than a neutral feeling about it, that's a different animal entirely.

And if you have any history with disordered eating, "I stopped thinking about food" deserves a real conversation with someone qualified, not a comment section. The same silence can mean two completely opposite things depending on who's experiencing it.

If you're on GLP-1 medication, taking any prescription, or managing a diagnosed condition including an eating disorder, this stuff needs actual medical supervision. Appetite changes on top of medication changes can move fast. Don't make calls based on a blog post or a thread. Your prescriber needs to be in the loop.

What people say actually helps

The advice that keeps surfacing from folks who've been through it is pretty grounded.

Eat on a schedule for a while. If hunger cues went quiet, you may not get the reminder. Loose structure beats waiting for a signal that isn't coming.

Track for a week, not forever. Just to confirm you're actually eating enough. Then stop, because tracking has its own noise problem.

Move the ritual, don't delete it. If food was your connection thing, the connection still needs somewhere to go. Cook for other people. Keep showing up to dinner. Make the coffee a thing.

Find the new hobby. Sounds glib, it isn't. A surprising number of people say the flat feeling lifted once something filled the space.

Give it time before you panic. Plenty of people describe the noise settling into something more normal after a few months. Not roaring back, not dead silent. Just quieter.

The takeaway

Food noise going quiet isn't automatically good or bad. It's information. The question isn't whether the radio turned off, it's what's playing now that it did.

If the answer is "nothing, and it's peaceful," great. That's the version everyone hopes for. If the answer is "nothing, and it's kind of hollow," you're not broken and you're definitely not the only one. That post gets written every week by someone who thinks they're the first.

I'm not the only one who found the silence weirder than expected. Say so out loud. Somebody in the comments has been waiting for permission to admit the same thing.

I'm not a doctor. I'm someone who's deep in this community and reads basically everything. Take all the health stuff with a grain of salt, pun fully intended. I can tell you what people are experiencing and what's trending, but you've got to make your own calls with someone qualified. I'm here for the real tea, not medical advice.
