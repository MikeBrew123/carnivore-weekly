Carnivore and Cardio: Eating Right When Running in Heat

2026-05-15 by Marcus Cole

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only, not medical advice. Details

Fat-Adapted Running in the Heat: What Actually Changes

May means outdoor training. Heat, humidity, and longer runs. If you're carnivore and adding cardio, your fueling strategy needs to be dialed in before you start logging miles.

Here's what changes in the heat and exactly what to do about it.

What Heat Does to a Fat-Adapted Runner

Fat-adapted athletes burn fat more efficiently than carb-fueled runners. That's the upside. But heat adds two complications: electrolyte loss accelerates, and perceived effort increases even at the same pace.

What happens physiologically in the heat:

  • Sweat rate increases. You lose sodium, potassium, and magnesium faster.
  • Core temperature rises faster, pulling more blood to the skin for cooling.
  • Fat oxidation stays high, but performance ceiling drops if electrolytes aren't replaced.
  • Thirst signals lag. You can be 2% dehydrated before you feel it.

On a carnivore diet, you're already running lower glycogen stores. That's fine for fat-adapted running. But it means electrolyte management isn't optional. It's the variable that separates a strong training day from a bad one.

CGM on Carnivore covers how blood glucose behaves during fat-adapted exercise if you want the data behind this.

The Adaptation Timeline: What to Expect Weeks 1 Through 5+

Be honest with yourself about where you are in adaptation. Your expectations need to match your timeline.

Weeks 1-4 (early adaptation):

  • Performance will drop. This is normal and temporary.
  • Easy runs feel harder. Pace slows by 30-90 seconds per mile on average.
  • Fatigue hits earlier in longer sessions.
  • Your body is still building the fat-burning machinery. Give it time.
  • Heat amplifies this. Keep runs short. 30-45 minutes max.

Weeks 5+ (fat-adapted):

  • Pace returns to baseline. Many athletes report improvements beyond their carb-fueled numbers.
  • Energy is steadier across the full run. No bonking, no mid-run crashes.
  • Heat tolerance improves. Electrolyte management becomes intuitive.
  • Long runs up to 90 minutes require zero mid-run food if pre-run nutrition is right.

Don't quit in week 3. That's the hardest week. It's also the week before it gets noticeably better.

Heat Acclimation: The Two-Week On-Ramp

Fat adaptation and heat adaptation are two different processes, and people confuse them every May. You can be 12 weeks carnivore and still fall apart on the first 80F morning, because your body hasn't rebuilt its cooling system yet.

The good news: heat acclimation is fast, it's free, and it's the biggest performance lever you have in summer. Most of it happens inside two weeks of consistent exposure. Plasma volume climbs, you start sweating earlier and more efficiently, and your heart rate at a given pace comes back down.

Here's the on-ramp. Run it in the heat you're actually going to train in, not on a treadmill in air conditioning.

  • Days 1-3: 20 to 30 minutes, easy effort, in the heat. That's the whole session. It will feel insultingly short. Do it anyway.
  • Days 4-7: 30 to 45 minutes, still easy. Pace is irrelevant this week. You're training the cooling system, not the engine.
  • Days 8-11: 45 to 60 minutes easy, plus one short pickup near the end, four or five minutes at moderate effort. First taste of intensity.
  • Days 12-14: Back to your normal duration. Add a real moderate block of 10 to 15 minutes if the first 11 days went well.
  • After two weeks: Resume normal programming. Keep at least three sessions a week in the heat or you'll lose the adaptation in roughly the time it took to build it.

The rules that make it work:

  • Go by effort, not pace. Your summer easy pace is slower. That's the adaptation working, not you getting worse.
  • Run at the same time of day while you're acclimating. Consistency is the active ingredient.
  • Back off a day if your resting heart rate is up in the morning, you slept badly, or you're coming off an illness. Heat punishes all three.
  • Don't stack a new diet, a new training block and a heat block in the same fortnight. Change one thing at a time so you know what caused what.

Skipping the on-ramp is how people get into trouble in June. Nobody gets heat illness in week six of summer. They get it on the first hot day they treated like a normal day.

Two limits sit on top of all of this. When there's a heat advisory or the humidity is extreme, that day becomes a rest day or an indoor day, not a shorter run outside. And learn the warning signs before your first session rather than during it: confusion, chills or goosebumps in hot weather, skin that goes hot and dry, or sweating that stops while you're still hot. Those mean stop and get help, not push through.

Pre-Run Nutrition Protocol

2-3 hours before a run:

  • 3-4 eggs cooked in butter
  • 4-6 oz ground beef or bacon
  • Salt your food to taste
  • Water, enough that you start the run already hydrated

30-60 minutes before a run:

  • No solid food. Your gut will thank you.
  • Water. An electrolyte drink only if you've cleared one with your doctor or pharmacist.

Fasted running (advanced): Once fully fat-adapted (6+ weeks), easy runs under 45 minutes work well fasted. Keep intensity low. This is not for beginners or weeks 1-4.

During the Run: Hydration and Electrolytes

Hydration by run length:

  • Under 45 minutes: carry water if it's hot and drink when you're thirsty. No food needed.
  • 45-75 minutes: carry water and plan a drink stop. Still no food needed.
  • 75-90 minutes: carry more than you think you'll need and drink to thirst, not to a timer.
  • 90+ minutes: same rule, and most fat-adapted runners still don't need food. Don't force fluid down on a schedule either. Drinking well past thirst on a long hot run carries its own risk.

Plan the route around water, not willpower. In summer, run loops instead of point-to-point. A three-mile loop past your car, your front door or a park fountain means you're never more than 15 minutes from a top-up and never committed to finishing a run that has gone wrong. Map the fountains once in May and you'll use that route all season.

Run the clock, not the calendar. Before 7am and after 8pm are a different sport from noon. Humidity matters more than the number on the thermometer, because sweat that drips instead of evaporating is cooling nobody. If the only slot you have is the middle of the day, cut the duration and drop the intensity instead of pretending the heat isn't there.

In the heat, fluid matters more than any product. Whether you need electrolytes in that fluid, and how much, depends on your sweat rate, the weather and your own health history. Get that answer from your doctor or pharmacist, especially if you take blood pressure or diuretic medication, have kidney or heart disease, or you're pregnant.

If you do use a product, read the label first. Check the sodium per serving and what the brand counts as a serving. Check the potassium, because that's the ingredient that interacts with heart and blood pressure medication. Skip anything with sugar or maltodextrin high in the ingredient list, since that's a sports drink in a smaller sachet. Treat "lite salt" or "salt substitute" as a potassium supplement, because it is one. And don't mix your own brew from a recipe you found online, including an old one of mine. Homemade potassium and magnesium mixes are the easiest way to get this badly wrong.

Track the conditions, not the milligrams. Four things in the log after a hot run: temperature, how long you were out, how you felt in the last 10 minutes, and whether your kit dried out crusted with salt. Six weeks of that tells you which conditions wreck you personally, which is better information than any average.

One more thing, because heat is the variable that turns a training run into an ambulance ride. Stop and get help if you become confused, stop sweating while you're still hot, get chills or goosebumps in hot weather, or your skin goes hot and dry. That is not a day to push.

Post-Run Recovery Protocol

Within 30 minutes of finishing:

  • 20-30g of protein, which is roughly a quarter pound of ground beef or three eggs. Real food, nothing special required. If a clinician has already set a protein limit for you, that number wins over this one.
  • Salt your food to taste, and keep drinking.
  • Water, steadily, over the next couple of hours.

Within 2 hours:

  • Full carnivore meal. Don't skip this. Heat training burns through protein faster.
  • Add extra fat. Butter, tallow, fatty cuts. Fat is your fuel source. Replenish it.

How Heat Affects Fat Oxidation

In extreme heat above 85F, fat oxidation efficiency drops slightly because your cardiovascular system is working harder to regulate temperature. You're not burning carbs instead. You're just working harder at the same pace.

The fix is simple: slow down. Your easy pace in the summer should be 15-30 seconds per mile slower than your easy pace in cooler weather. This isn't weakness. It's physics.

Heart rate targets for heat training:

  • Easy run: 65-75% max heart rate
  • Moderate effort: 75-83% max heart rate
  • Stay out of zone 4 and 5 until temps drop below 75F

High-intensity intervals in the heat during early fat adaptation is a recipe for a bad time. Save speed work for cool mornings or fall.

For more on how carnivore supports strength alongside endurance, check Strength Gains Without Carbs. The principles on muscle preservation apply directly to runners maintaining training volume in summer.

Fat adaptation doesn't care about the heat. Your body does. Respect the temperature, drink, slow down, and the rest follows.

Not a doctor. I've coached people and competed myself, so I know what works in practice. But I'm not your doctor. Heat is the one variable that turns a training session into an emergency. Stop and get help for confusion, chills or goosebumps in hot weather, hot skin that has stopped sweating, or a headache that won't clear with fluids. If you take blood pressure or diuretic medication, or you have a heart condition, set your heat and sodium plan with your doctor before summer training. Your mileage may vary.
