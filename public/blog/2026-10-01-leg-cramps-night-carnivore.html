Leg Cramps at Night on Carnivore: What Helps

2026-10-01 by Sarah Whitfield

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only, not medical advice. Details

You're finally asleep, and then your calf locks into a hard knot. Or your toes curl under and won't let go. If this started in your first few weeks of carnivore, you're in good company. Night leg and foot cramps are one of the most common early complaints I hear about.

Here's the short answer. Most early carnivore cramps come from losing water and sodium fast, often with magnesium running low too. For most people, spreading salt through the day, adding a small salty drink before bed, and taking magnesium in the evening settles them within a week or two. If you take blood pressure pills, a water pill, or heart medication, or you have kidney disease or heart failure, please read the safety section below before you change anything.

Why cramps show up in weeks 1-3

When you cut carbs, insulin drops. Lower insulin tells your kidneys to let go of sodium, and water follows the sodium out. That's a big part of the 3-7 lb most people lose in the first week. It's real progress, but it pulls electrolytes out with it.

Nerves and muscles need sodium, potassium and magnesium to fire and then relax. When those run low, or you're a bit dehydrated, a muscle can fire and not let go.

A few other things stack on top of that:

  • Age. Night cramps get more common after 50 for everyone, carnivore or not. Muscles and tendons shorten a little, and many of us are less active in the evening.
  • Magnesium. Plenty of adults run low on it before they ever change their diet, and the early water loss can make that show.
  • Some medications. Diuretics and statins can both be linked to muscle cramps. More on that below.

I've written about the full electrolyte picture before, so I won't repeat it here. If you want the whole protocol with the reasoning behind each number, it's in our carnivore electrolyte dosing guide. This post is just about the cramps.

First, if you take medication or have kidney or heart trouble

This part matters more than any number in this article. If you take blood pressure medication, a diuretic ("water pill"), or any heart medication, or you've been told you have kidney disease or heart failure, please don't add salt, magnesium or potassium on your own. Ask your doctor first.

The amounts below are written for healthy adults. They're not meant for you to follow on your own, because your body may handle these minerals very differently. Too much potassium or magnesium can build up when kidneys aren't clearing them well, and extra salt can work against your heart or blood pressure treatment.

Also, carnivore often lowers blood pressure by itself. If you're on blood pressure pills and you're cramping, feeling dizzy when you stand, or wiped out, those can be signs your dose needs a look. We cover that in what to do when your blood pressure drops too low.

One more thing. If you take a diuretic or a statin and you're cramping, tell your prescriber. That's worth reporting. It's not a reason to stop the medication yourself. Your doctor can sort out whether it's the drug, the diet change, or both, and decide what to adjust.

What helps for most healthy adults

Spread your sodium through the day. Our general range is 3-5 g of sodium a day for most adults. That's sodium, not salt. One teaspoon of salt is about 2.3 g of sodium. Salt your food well at each meal instead of taking it all at once.

Try a small salty drink before bed. A pinch of salt in a small glass of water, or a mug of salted bone broth, is a fine fix for night cramps. Some people find this one change stops the cramps within a night or two.

Take magnesium in the evening. Magnesium glycinate, 300-400 mg in the evening, is what I've seen work best. Skip magnesium oxide, which your body absorbs poorly.

Get potassium from food first. Meat covers most of what you need. Beef, pork, salmon and bone broth all carry potassium. I'm not giving potassium supplement doses here, and you shouldn't take large amounts on your own. Too much potassium is genuinely dangerous.

Drink to thirst, and a little more at first. You don't need to force gallons. Plain water with no added salt can dilute what you have, so pair your water with salted food or broth.

If cramps are tied up with poor sleep, the two often share a cause. Our post on week-two sleep and electrolytes walks through that link.

What to do when a cramp hits

You'll want to grab something that works in the moment:

  • Flex your foot toward you. Pull your toes up toward your shin. That stretches the calf and usually breaks the cramp.
  • Stand up and walk. Putting weight on the leg and taking a few steps often releases it faster than lying still.
  • Rub the muscle. Gentle massage helps it relax.
  • Stretch before bed. A 30-second calf stretch against the wall on each side, most nights, is worth trying.

How long does this last?

For most people, early cramps fade as the body adapts, usually within the first two to four weeks. The roughest stretch tends to be days 3-10. Once your sodium and magnesium are steady, many people find the cramps stop within a few days.

If they keep coming back after that, look at your salt habits first. KetoDial's cramp troubleshooting decision tree is a handy step-by-step check that works just as well on carnivore. Our post on the carnivore electrolyte problem covers the usual reasons people stay short.

When to call your doctor

Most night cramps are annoying, not dangerous. But please get checked if you notice any of these:

  • Swelling, warmth or redness in one calf, with pain. That can be a blood clot. Get seen the same day, and go to urgent care or the ER if you can't reach your doctor.
  • Real muscle weakness, not just soreness after a cramp.
  • Dark, tea-colored urine, especially with muscle pain.
  • Cramps that last past your first month even after you've fixed salt, magnesium and fluids.

Your situation might be different from the typical pattern, and that's what your doctor is there for. Bring a short note of when the cramps happen, what you're eating, and what you take. That makes the visit much more useful.

Not a doctor: This article is general information, not medical advice. If you have a health condition or take medication, talk with your doctor before changing your diet.
