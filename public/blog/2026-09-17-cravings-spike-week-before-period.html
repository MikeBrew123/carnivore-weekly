Why Cravings Spike the Week Before Your Period

2026-09-17 by Sarah Whitfield

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only, not medical advice. Details

If your cravings go from manageable to unmanageable about a week before your period, you're not imagining it and you haven't lost your discipline. The luteal phase, the stretch between ovulation and the first day of bleeding, shifts your hormones in ways that raise appetite and make food harder to ignore. That happens on keto, on carnivore, and on a normal diet too.

Here's the direct answer: eating low carb doesn't switch off the hormonal half of your cycle. What it does is take away the blood sugar rollercoaster that used to sit on top of it, so the craving shows up without the crash that made it twice as loud. The way through isn't white knuckling. It's more protein, enough fat, more salt, and patience while the window closes on its own.

What's actually happening the week before your period

After ovulation, progesterone climbs and estrogen falls. Progesterone does most of the work here. It nudges your resting metabolic rate up a little, so you genuinely need more food than you did two weeks ago. Cycle research puts that bump at roughly 100 to 300 calories a day, which is small on paper and very noticeable in real life.

Insulin sensitivity dips slightly in the same window. Serotonin drops with estrogen, which is why the pull is toward comfort food and not a steak. Progesterone also fragments sleep, and short sleep raises hunger hormones the next day. So you're hungrier, less regulated, and more tired at once. That isn't a character flaw.

Then your period starts, progesterone falls off a cliff, and appetite often disappears. Many women describe eating enormous amounts for most of a week and then not caring about food at all. That whiplash is normal. Research shows general patterns, though, and your body may run its own version.

Why low carb changes the shape, not the schedule

I covered the broader hormone picture in Women's Carnivore Health: The Honest Truth About Hormones, so I won't repeat it. What's worth adding is that steady glucose changes what a craving feels like. Without the crash, it's signal and habit, not physical shakiness. Louder in your head, quieter in your hands.

The two diets behave a little differently here. On keto, some women find their usual carb allowance feels less forgiving in the luteal phase, with more water retention. On carnivore there's no carb dial to fuss with, which is the real relief for some people. Carnivore vs Keto lays out the tradeoffs, and the keto side of cycle questions gets more room in why luteal week wrecks your numbers.

Why perimenopause makes it louder and less predictable

In your forties and early fifties, estrogen stops moving in a smooth curve. It spikes higher and drops faster, sometimes inside the same week. Cycles get shorter, then longer, then skip. So counting days and bracing yourself stops working, because the window arrives early, or twice, or not at all.

Progesterone also runs lower in perimenopause, partly because more cycles happen without ovulation. Lower progesterone means worse sleep and more sensitivity to low sodium and low magnesium, which is the exact combination that turns a mild craving into a 9 p.m. hunt through the kitchen. Track symptoms instead of dates. Two bad nights plus sore breasts plus a short temper tells you more than a calendar app.

The answer is protein, fat, salt, and time

Protein first. Aim for 30 to 40 grams per meal rather than one giant dose at dinner, which matters more after 50 than it did at 30. Across the day, 0.8 to 1.0 grams per pound of your goal body weight is the range we use. In your luteal week, add protein to the meal just before your usual crash time instead of holding the line all day and losing at night. How Much Protein Do You Actually Need on Carnivore? has the details, and if you want a starting point for your own numbers, the free macro calculator gives you one in a couple of minutes.

Fat is the lever. A rough satiety baseline is about 1:1 fat to protein by grams. If you're in a fat loss phase and normally trim added butter or tallow, leave it in this week. Trim added fat when you're cutting, never the protein, and don't cut anything during the days your body is asking for more.

Salt does more here than people expect. Most adults do well on 3 to 5 grams of sodium a day, and 1 teaspoon of salt gives about 2.3 grams. Spread it out rather than loading one meal. If you wake at 3 a.m., a small salty drink before bed is a reasonable fix. Magnesium and potassium need a caution before anything else. Both are cleared by your kidneys, and both interact with common prescriptions. If you take blood pressure medication, a diuretic, anything for your heart, or you have reduced kidney function, ask your pharmacist or doctor before you add either one, because the safe amount depends on you and we won't print a number. Potassium you don't have to chase at all: meat carries it, and food is the safe route. If magnesium is worth trying for sleep and cramping, glycinate absorbs well, oxide doesn't, and evening is the useful time to take it. Electrolytes on Carnivore covers the rest.

Then time. The window runs five to seven days, and appetite usually drops the day bleeding starts.

About the swap you're considering

Some people find a few bites of a low carb frozen treat gets them through the evening. I understand the appeal, and I won't recommend it, because a swap doesn't close the craving loop. It keeps it open and negotiable for another day. Not with berries, not with dark chocolate, not with sugar free ice cream or candy.

And eating more meat this week isn't cheating. If you're on a no cheat stretch and you ate a pound and a half of ground beef on a Tuesday because your body demanded it, you didn't break anything. You answered a hormonal signal with food that doesn't restart the loop.

When this is worth a doctor's appointment

If the cravings come with mood changes bad enough to affect your work or your relationships, say that out loud to your doctor. PMDD is treatable and doesn't respond to dietary discipline. Heavy perimenopausal periods can also drop iron and ferritin, and low iron drives strange, insistent food urges, so a ferritin test is a simple thing to ask for. I live with an inherited blood condition that keeps my own baseline low, so I've learned not to guess at those numbers.

If you take medications or have a diagnosed condition, this general information isn't a substitute for medical care. Work with your provider before changing anything, especially blood sugar, thyroid, or blood pressure medication, since those doses interact with cycle changes and with a lower carb intake.

I'm not a doctor. I've researched this deeply and worked with many people, but I'm not your doctor. If you have health conditions, take medications, or need specific guidance, talk to someone who knows your full medical picture. Everything I write is educational based on research and what I've seen work. Your situation might be different.
