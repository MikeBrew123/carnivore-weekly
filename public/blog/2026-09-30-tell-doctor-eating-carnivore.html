How to Tell Your Doctor You're Eating Carnivore

2026-09-30 by Chloe Navarro

This post may contain affiliate links. For educational purposes only, not medical advice. Details

Okay, so here's the short answer. The easiest way to tell your doctor you're eating carnivore is to say it plainly at the start of the appointment, hand over a one-page written summary of what you actually eat, and ask one question: "What should we measure, and when?" That turns a nervous confession into a shared plan.

And if you take medication, especially for blood pressure or diabetes, telling your doctor isn't optional. A big change in how you eat can change how those medications work, and any adjustment is your prescriber's call. Not yours, not mine, not the internet's.

Why so many of us put this off

Real talk: a lot of people in this community are months into carnivore before they mention it at a checkup. Some people find they rehearse the conversation in the car, then chicken out and say "I've been eating healthier" instead. I get it. Nobody wants to feel like they're being scolded in a paper gown.

The nerves usually come from picturing the worst version of the conversation. In practice, many people find their doctor is more curious than cranky, especially once there's something concrete to look at. What makes them uneasy isn't usually the diet itself. It's not knowing what you're doing.

If you've already braced yourself for pushback at home, you know the feeling. Our post on what to do when your spouse thinks the diet will hurt you covers a lot of the same ground: less arguing, more information.

How people actually bring it up

You don't need a speech. A few openers that tend to go well:

  • "Before we start, I want you to know I've changed how I eat, and I'd like your help keeping an eye on it."
  • "I've been eating mostly meat, eggs and some dairy since March. I brought a summary so you can see exactly what that looks like."
  • "I'm thinking about trying a very low carb, mostly animal-based diet. Can we get some baseline labs first?"

Notice what all three have in common. They invite the doctor in. You're not asking permission to be an adult, and you're not asking for a debate. You're asking for a partner, which is exactly what a good doctor wants to be.

What to bring: the one-page summary

This is the single most useful thing you can do. Appointments are short, and "I eat a lot of meat" can mean anything. A written page answers the questions before they're asked. Keep it to one side of paper, big readable font, and include:

  • A typical day of eating. For example: breakfast is three eggs and bacon, lunch is a hamburger patty with cheese, dinner is a steak or pork chops. Note drinks too, like coffee or tea.
  • Your start date. Or your planned start date if you haven't begun yet.
  • Your weight. Where you started and where you are now, with dates.
  • Any home readings. Blood pressure, blood glucose, or anything else you track. A simple list of dates and numbers is perfect.
  • Every medication and supplement you take. Including the ones you think don't matter.
  • How you've been feeling. Energy, sleep, digestion, dizziness, anything new.
  • Your questions. Written down, so the nerves can't erase them.

Here's the thing: a doctor handed a tidy page like this tends to take you seriously.

Questions worth asking

You don't need to walk in with a stack of studies. These questions do more good than any printout:

  • "What should we measure, and when?"
  • "Is anything I take affected by eating very low carb?"
  • "What home readings should I keep tracking, and what numbers should make me call you?"
  • "If my cholesterol changes, which follow-up tests would you want to talk about?"

That last one matters. Cholesterol on carnivore is honestly unsettled, and it's a conversation to have with your doctor, not a fight to win against them. If your panel has already come back with numbers that worry you, KetoDial's guide to reading keto bloodwork is a calm place to start.

Why this matters most if you're on medication

This is the part I really want you to hear. Many people find that eating very low carb can lower blood pressure and blood sugar, sometimes fairly quickly. That sounds like great news, and it can be. But if you're on medication for either one, the dose you were prescribed was set for the old you, eating the old way.

Blood pressure medication on top of a diet that's already lowering your pressure can leave you dizzy or lightheaded. We wrote about that in what to do when your blood pressure drops too low. Diabetes medications, especially insulin and some pills, can push blood sugar too low when carbs drop. That can be dangerous.

So your prescriber may need to adjust things as the diet changes your numbers. That's their decision. Please don't cut back, skip, or stop anything on your own because your readings look better. Your home readings are how you help your doctor make that call well. The same goes for salt, potassium or magnesium supplements: if you take blood pressure, heart or water pills, or you have kidney or heart problems, ask your doctor before adding any of them.

Ask for baseline labs before you start

If you haven't started yet, this is the smartest move you can make. Ask for bloodwork before you change anything. A lipid panel, blood sugar markers and kidney function are common ones, but let your doctor decide what fits you.

Why bother? Because a result three months in only means something if you know where you started. Our post on high creatinine on carnivore is a good example of why context changes everything when you read a lab result.

If the first conversation doesn't go well

Sometimes a doctor is skeptical. That's okay. Skeptical isn't the same as wrong, and it doesn't mean they're against you. Often it just means they want to see data, and your summary and follow-up labs give them exactly that.

If you truly can't build a working partnership over time, a second opinion is a normal, reasonable option. But the goal is never to find someone who'll say yes to everything. The goal is someone who'll watch your numbers with you.

The nerves are real, and they're normal. But you and your doctor want the same thing: you, healthier, with numbers to prove it. One page of paper and one good question go a long way.

Not a doctor: This article is general information, not medical advice. If you have a health condition or take medication, talk with your doctor before changing your diet.
